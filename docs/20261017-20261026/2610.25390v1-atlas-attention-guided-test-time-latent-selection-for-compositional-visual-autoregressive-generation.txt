# ATLAS: Attention-Guided Test-Time Latent Selection for Compositional Visual Autoregressive Generation 

Hossein Shahabadi Raya Namazi Mahdieh Soleymani Baghshah Sharif University of Technology 

hosseinshahabadi.me@gmail.com 

## Abstract 

Visual autoregressive models produce convincing images but can misassign attributes and spatial relations. Searching multiple generation paths can improve composition, yet com-pleting every candidate is costly. We introduce ATLAS, a training-free framework that uses the frozen generator’s internal attention to guide search before final images are available. Within-prompt diagnostics identify attention-derived scores that predict final compositional preferences on Infinity, with category-dependent transfer to Switti. ATLAS uses these on-the-fly signals to progressively concentrate computation on promising trajectories. It maintains multiple coarse alternatives, prunes candidates without decoding at intermediate scales, and reserves image-space evaluation for a final choice among partial trajectories. ATLAS scales effectively on both backbones, achieving higher mean scores on the evaluated T2I-CompBench and GenEval categories as more computation is devoted to search. At the largest tested budget, the targeted T2I-CompBench mean improves by over 10% relative to each backbone. Separate profiles show over 50% less generation time than completing and evaluating all candidates at equal initial counts, alongside lower peak allocated GPU memory. Relative time savings are larger at the higher tested candidate count. Together, these results support scalable compositional search: additional sampling expands explo-ration, while attention-guided selection limits continuation cost without parameter updates or gradient-based optimization. 

## 1 Introduction 

Diffusion models generate images by iterative denoising (Rombach et al., 2022); visual autoregressive (VAR) models predict tokens across progressively finer scales (Tian et al., 2024). Infinity extends next-scale prediction to high-resolution text-to-image synthesis, while Switti uses a distinct scale-wise transformer design (Han et al., 2025; Voronov et al., 2024). Both expose intermediate states that can be inspected before generation is complete. High visual quality does not ensure correct composition. An image may misassign an attribute, omit an entity, or reverse a relation. T2I-CompBench++ and GenEval evaluate such errors (Huang et al., 2025; Ghosh et al., 2023; Shahabadi et al., 2025). Sampling alternatives creates opportunities for better composition, but completing every alternative incurs the full generation cost. Early selection avoids some of that work only if an intermediate score identifies paths worth continuing. Test-time interventions address composition through steering or search. VISTA steers intermediate representa-tions with attention constraints, and SynVAR guides spatial and semantic generation (Shahabadi et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026). TTS-VAR preserves coarse diversity before potential-based path selection (Chen et al., 2025b); related next-token and raster-scan methods use token confidence and progressive grid partitions (Chen et al., 2025a; Park et al., 2026). Attention offers another selection signal by connecting prompt tokens to visual positions without decoding candidates. Its use for steering does not establish whether it predicts which sampled trajectory will finish better. 1arXiv:2610.25390v1 [cs.CV] 18 Oct 2026 We ask whether a frozen generator’s attention can allocate search computation toward better compositional outcomes. We choose losses and layers on calibration prompts, then test their predictions among held-out candidates for the same prompt. Infinity scores achieve 72.9% pairwise agreement for planar relations and 62.7% for color binding over scale indices 2–6, against 50% chance. Selected color and texture losses also rank Switti candidates above chance by their prompt-bootstrap intervals. Scale and layer analyses characterize signal availability; a separate prompt-switch experiment provides context on early visual commitments. Attention-Guided Test-Time Latent Selection (ATLAS) turns intermediate scores into a search policy. It retains several coarse candidates, applies three attention-guided cuts, and decodes the remaining partial images for one ImageReward decision (Xu et al., 2023). Only one survivor completes the finer scales. This division uses internal scores for repeated selection and reserves image-space evaluation for the final commitment. The generator remains frozen, with no parameter updates or gradient-based latent optimization. To our knowledge, ATLAS is the first attention-guided compositional search framework evaluated across two distinct VAR model families. End-to-end T2I-CompBench and GenEval averages increase with search budget on Infinity and Switti. Separate profiles with 8 or 16 initial candidates show 52–73% less generation time than completing and evaluating the same number, with lower peak allocated memory. These results assess the complete controller; the diagnostic study separately establishes the predictive value of its attention-based scores. Our contributions are as follows: • We study when prompt changes affect next-scale generation and how attention-derived compositional signals vary with scale, layer selection, and constraint category. • We evaluate those signals through within-prompt final-image preferences, freeze loss and layer choices before held-out testing, and test their transfer from Infinity to Switti. • We introduce ATLAS, a training-free controller that combines internal attention pruning with one image-space selection stage, and evaluate its compositional scores across search budgets alongside generation time and GPU memory.

## 3 What Does Attention Reveal Before an Image Is Complete? 

An intermediate score is useful only if it distinguishes continuations worth computing. We examine this requirement in two ways. Prompt switches probe whether early prefixes constrain the eventual image. Completed same-prompt candidates then provide a direct test of attention-based prediction, including its dependence on scale and layer choice.

3.3 Layer choice determines which attention an objective reads 

Calibration compares 15 contiguous layer selections to identify which maps support ranking. Figure 3 summarizes layers 1–8, 9–16, and all 32. The selected blocks are 1–8 for binding/depth and 9–16 for planar relations; depth broadens the best four-layer choice for simplicity (Appendix A). 

Fixed-loss comparisons retain much of the calibrated layer pattern. With each loss held fixed, the selected block has the highest held-out agreement in four Infinity categories; texture instead favors all layers (Table 2). Switti point estimates likewise favor layers 1–8 for binding/depth and 9–16 for planar relations. Agreement extends beyond the selected loss: Top-3 and Top-5 mean accuracies exceed 50% by point estimate in every category (Table 2). These averages of per-loss metrics summarize several objectives; they are not candidate-score ensembles. Layer preference also depends on the objective. Across the full loss bank, held-out averages favor early layers for color and all layers for texture; planar correlation favors early layers even though Separation ranks best with layers 9–16 (Figure 4). The useful choice is therefore a loss–layer pairing, rather than a layer block alone. Together, prompt switches reveal persistent early visual commitments, and within-prompt comparisons show that selected attention losses can rank trajectories before completion. The depth patterns are consistent with early attention organizing entity, attribute, and depth-related regions, while further processing benefits some planar scores. ATLAS turns this predictive information into a search policy, using attention to decide which paths receive the remaining computation. 

## 4 ATLAS: Attention-Guided Test-Time Latent Selection 

Turning attention-based ranking into computational savings requires deciding which paths survive, how many remain, and when to commit to one. ATLAS uses category-specific scores for three cuts without decoding, a descending candidate budget, and one ImageReward decision to choose the path to complete (Figure 5).

Attention controls the first three reductions; ImageReward makes the final choice. Our benchmark label N = m multiplies the base eight-path schedule: 8 N initial paths narrow to 2 N finalists, preserving the pruning proportions. ## 5 Compositional Quality and Inference Cost 

We test whether ATLAS turns intermediate selection into better final composition and lower search cost. Benchmarks follow standard protocols with four seeds, except the single-run Infinity BoN baseline and random-pruning ablation. 5.1 T2I-CompBench 

ATLAS improves targeted compositional scores as candidate budget grows. The backbone comparison tests whether more ATLAS computation improves composition (Table 4). Relative Avg. 6 gains are 4 .3%, 7 .3%, and 10 .5% on Infinity and 5 .2%, 7 .7%, and 10 .6% on Switti at budget multipliers N = 1 , 2, 8, using unrounded means. Complex composition improves at every budget; numeracy and non-spatial outputs remain unchanged. Both backbones therefore benefit from scaling this search policy. Appendix G reports run-level variability. 

ATLAS reaches competitive compositional quality across both backbones. TTS-VAR and BoN compare ATLAS with alternative uses of additional sampling. On Infinity, TTS-VAR has higher averages, while ATLAS leads on texture and complex composition at both shared budgets and on color at N = 2. Switti ATLAS approaches BoN’s averages at its largest setting, although ATLAS starts 64 paths and BoN completes eight. ## 7 Conclusion 

Internal attention can guide compositional search before final images are available. Within-prompt diagnostics identify predictive binding and spatial scores, with category-dependent transfer across backbones. ATLAS turns these on-the-fly signals into training-free selection, retaining promising trajectories while reducing the number that require further computation. Compositional benchmark means rise with search budget on both Infinity and Switti. Separate profiles show lower generation time and peak allocated memory than completing all candidates at equal initial counts. ## A Diagnostic Evaluation Protocol 

Records and settings. Infinity calibration has 100 records per category; each backbone has 50 held-out prompts per category, with seeds 1000–1002. Records join by prompt and seed. The 52 losses comprise nine planar, eleven per binding category, and ten depth definitions. Layers are one-based; recorded scales 2–8 are zero-based. Reference settings use bfloat16 inference. Infinity-2B uses its associated visual tokenizer and Flan-T5-XL text encoder, guidance 3, temperature 0.5, top-k 900, and top-p 0.97. ScaleKV uses window/capacity/kernel 8/400 /5 with max pooling. Switti uses the 1024-resolution model, guidance 6, top-k 400, top-p 0.95, and disables guidance from index 10. 

Preferences and exclusions. After deduplicating stored calls and mapping display positions to seeds, agreeing winners or a winner plus a tie define a directional pair. Conflicting winners and all-tie pairs are excluded. Missing matches and effective loss ties (relative tolerance 10 −7, absolute tolerance 10 −12 ) are also excluded. Signed 2D scores use absolute value; other frozen scores use identity. Correlation and single-winner selection require a preference score for each candidate. Each candidate faces the other two candidates in both display orders, giving four judgments. We award one point for a win, half a point for a tie, and zero for a loss, then average these four values. Unlike pairwise agreement, this score retains the individual judgments even when reversing image order changes the winner. At each prompt and scale, selection chooses the candidate with the lowest attention loss. We evaluate it when losses are available for all three candidates, the minimum loss is unique, and the VLM preference scores are not all equal. Selection is correct if the chosen candidate has the highest preference score, including a shared highest score. Its random baseline is the number of VLM-best candidates divided by three, averaged over eligible selections. In the tables, Top-1 loss means the single objective chosen during calibration; selection accuracy measures how often that objective chooses a VLM-best candidate. 13 Aggregation and calibration. Per-prompt Spearman and Kendall’s τb require complete, nonconstant values. Prompt-centered Spearman subtracts each prompt’s mean loss and preference before pooling candidates; figures display its negative. Layer figures average scale-wise metrics within windows 2–3, 2–4, 2–6, and 2–8, then equally across windows and losses. Early scales therefore recur. Primary agreement instead pools evaluable pair–scale decisions (Equation 4). Calibration evaluates 15 layer selections and ranks losses by mean negative centered Spearman across those windows. ## C Attention-Loss Definitions and Sources 

This appendix provides context for the loss bank by summarizing objectives from prior work and the local variants evaluated in our study. SynGen motivates matching attributes to their nouns while separating unrelated pairs (Rassin et al., 2023); CONFORM supplies a contrastive formulation (Meral et al., 2024); and Divide&Bind supplies an attribute–noun binding objective (Li et al., 2023). SupCon draws on supervised con-trastive learning (Khosla et al., 2020). For spatial relations, PoS and PSE follow the probability-of-superiority approach of PSG (Rezaei et al., 2025), while the centroid implementation follows the spatial-violation construction of InfSplign (Rastegar et al., 2025). Separation is related to A-STAR’s attention-segregation principle (Agarwal et al., 2023), and boundary containment follows VISTA’s depth objective (Shahabadi et al., 2026). We summarize these objectives and their adaptations here, rather than introduce them as new contributions. The bank contains eleven binding losses evaluated separately for color, shape, and texture, nine planar losses, and ten depth losses: 52 category-specific definitions in total. C.2 Binding objectives 

Binding objectives measure whether an attribute and its noun attend to compatible regions. The local SynGen variant uses cosine similarity to favor matched pairs over unrelated nouns. KL and JSD compare their spatial attention distributions, while Divide&Bind aligns those distributions after rescaling the attribute map. CONFORM treats matched attribute–noun embeddings as positives and other pairs as negatives; Exclusive and SupCon instead score the correct noun against competing nouns. The remaining variants probe alternative matching statistics: Overlap measures Bhattacharyya overlap, Focal reweights cosine matching to emphasize poorly aligned pairs, and Peak compares soft centroids as defined above. The binding Composite equally averages the local SynGen and KL objectives. We evaluate these eleven losses separately on color, shape, and texture prompts. Our generation implementation uses SynGen with competing-noun weight 0.3 and CONFORM with temperature 0.07 and 3 × 3 Gaussian smoothing (σ = 0 .5). We include the objective implementations in the supplement. 

C.3 Planar objectives 

The nine planar objectives measure ordering, location, and separation. Centroid penalizes violations of the requested ordering between noun-map centers; Centroid-Prox adds similarity-based scoring for proximity relations. PoS and PSE use spatial distributions rather than centers to assess which noun lies on the requested side. Half-Field measures attention outside the requested image half, while Gaussian-Target compares it with a smooth target near the appropriate edge. Separation penalizes shared support. Our soft-box overlap objective, named IoU in the code, measures intersection area without dividing by union. Planar Composite combines separation and directional terms. Together, these variants test whether relative position or broader map geometry better predicts compositional preference. 16 C.4 Depth objectives 

The ten depth objectives use attention geometry as a proxy for foreground–background ordering. Footprint and Entropy compare coordinate spread and effective support size, respectively. Depth-Peak favors a stronger foreground attention peak; Depth-Overlap favors foreground dominance where both maps have mass. Vertical PSE tests whether the foreground noun lies lower in the image, a ground-plane heuristic rather than a direct depth measurement. Containment-Adjacency encourages the objects to be adjacent without merging their maps. Boundary Containment evaluates whether virtual points from the background boundary facing the foreground fall within foreground attention support. Depth Composite combines containment-adjacency, peak dominance, and overlap dominance. Separation and soft-box intersection complete the depth bank, providing relation-independent comparisons against these ordering-sensitive objectives. 

## D Controller Operation 

Attention scoring. The controller averages text-conditioned attention across heads and selected layers before computing each loss. If the selected layers are unavailable, it uses all captured layers. Benchmark annotations identify the constraints; free-form parsing supports at most one relation. Each run uses one binding objective and equally averages it with applicable planar and depth losses, without rescaling their values. CONFORM requires at least two attribute–noun pairs; a configured alternative can handle fewer. 

Pruning and final selection. At each scheduled pruning step, the controller retains the required number of lowest-loss candidates. Cutoff ties follow the sorting order, so the budget remains fixed; the offline diagnostic instead excludes tied comparisons. If attention maps or required loss terms are unavailable, the cut is skipped. The remaining finalists are decoded from their accumulated latents into full-resolution partial images, and ImageReward selects the trajectory to continue. Profiling decodes two finalists at a time to limit memory use.

## H Inference Profiling Protocol 

We profile equal initial candidate counts on an A100 40 GB, with 30 outputs per setting. We use color prompts, seed 1000, one discarded warm-up, two-candidate decoding batches, and expandable memory segments for every method. We keep Infinity’s text encoder on the GPU. Our timing code synchronizes CUDA before and after each generation call and resets peak-memory counters before timing. Initial model loading, attention-hook setup, and image-file writing lie outside that interval. We report mean generation time and maximum allocated and reserved memory across outputs, dividing peak bytes by 2 20 (MiB). ## I Broader Impact 

More efficient compositional generation can support visual content creation, but can also facilitate misleading imagery. Selection may amplify biases in the generator or reward model. Deployment should retain appropriate content safeguards and evaluate demographic bias and output diversity; compositional benchmark scores alone do not assess these risks. 18

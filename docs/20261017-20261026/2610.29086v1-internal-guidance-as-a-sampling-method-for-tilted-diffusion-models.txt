# Internal Guidance as a Sampling Method for Tilted Diffusion Models 

## Curtis McDonald 1 and Peter Bartlett 1,2 1Simons Institute, University of California Berkeley 

> 2

## Google DeepMind Oct 7, 2026 

Abstract 

Reward guidance methods sample an exponential tilt of a base density using a diffusion model trained with only samples from the base distribution. Existing methods use what we call external guidance: additive terms in the drift or multiplicative terms on the transition probabilities. We propose internal guidance which leaves the output of the base denoiser unmodified and instead changes the point at which it is evaluated. For discrete distributions embedded in the hypercube 

{− 1, 1}d, the denoiser of any linearly tilted density is exactly the base denoiser evaluated at a shifted and rescaled input. In practice the base denoiser is a neural network trained on noised samples of the base distribution. The required evaluation points for sampling the tilted density would be out-of-distribution for basic noising of the base distribution. We modify the training algorithm with random diagonal rescalings of the data, so that the denoiser network learns both in- and out-of-distribution evaluations of the denoiser relative to the base distribution. A single denoiser network samples binary reward tilts, class labels, and infilling with no fine-tuning and no external guidance term. 1

# 1 Introduction 

Diffusion models designed via Gaussian noising have become the dominant generative algorithm for continuous distributions [29, 13, 32], and continue to show promise for discrete targets as well [7, 16, 35]. The original theory of diffusion models was established for unconditional generation [31, 32]: given access to an initial pool of samples from a distribution π, we train a model to sample from 

π. As the field has matured, interest has turned towards conditional generation and tilting. Given access to only a pool of samples from π, and a reward function r, we want to train a sampling algorithm to sample from an exponential tilt by the reward function for some strength τ with density ˜ π(x) ∝ π(x)eτ r (x).Existing methods utilize what the authors refer to as external guidance , implementing an additive vector field called a guidance term to modify SDE paths at sampling time [10, 28, 11], or a density ratio to reweight Markov chain conditional probabilities [33, 24, 17, 12]. Methods such as classier-fee guidance [14] implement ad hoc and closed-form guidance terms, but are known to structurally sample the wrong invariant measure and introduce sampling artifacts such as corrupted samples and mode collapse [8, 4]. Exact guidance schemes derive the analytically correct guidance terms, but result in an intractable object that must either be learned in an expensive neural network  

> 1Code available at https://github.com/CMcDonald-1/internal_guidance_diffusion_models

1arXiv:2610.29086v1 [stat.ML] 21 Oct 2026 training run, or approximated by a tractable analog such as an empirical average that introduces compounding errors at sample time. A recent line of work [15, 21, 20, 22] has opened up a new method for reward-tilted sampling we call internal guidance . Rather than modify the output of the denoiser of the base distribution with additive or multiplicative terms outside of the object, we realize we can instead shift our evaluation point inside the existing base denoiser. Recent work of Moitra et al. [21, Lemma 3.3] has shown for an internal mapping function H we call a symmetry map, the evaluation of the denoiser of a linear tilted density is an evaluation of the base denoiser at a modified input 

Gtilt (u, a ) = Gbase (˜ u = H1(u, a ), ˜a = H2(u, a )) .

These symmetry mappings are closed-form objects involving shifting and rescaling inputs, derived from the interactions of linear tilts with the quadratic log-density terms introduced via Gaussian convolution. Internal guidance has clear benefits. There is no intractable external term to approximate, and the symmetry maps H are all explicit. A single denoising diffusion model, trained on unconditional sampling of its input distribution, is able to sample an entire family of tilted densities at inference time with no additional reinforcement learning, fine tuning, or external guidance required. The catch with this method is one of coverage. If we had access to the oracle denoiser G we could implement such a method with no additional error over base sampling. However, we instead have a network gθ trained to approximate G. This network is only accurate in an L2 sense along the sequence of marginal densities produced by noising the base distribution, which we call the training manifold. The equivalent points (˜ u, ˜a) induced by the symmetry map represent an out-of-distribution query from the perspective of the base model denoiser, and we have no control on the accuracy of our trained network at this point. The relationship of the symmetry map can be captured by an equivalence class: for a tilted density ˜ π and an evaluation point ( u, a ), the equivalence class is all points (˜ u, ˜a) where the base denoiser evaluated at (˜ u, ˜a) is equal to the tilted denoiser at ( u, a ). The work of Moitra et al. [21] can be interpreted as showing this equivalence class exists and is non-empty, and established one symmetry map which creates the class. However, this symmetry alone is not enough to force overlap between the equivalence class of points produced by tilted sampling and the high probability region of the base distribution training manifold the denoiser network was trained on. To have equivalent points that live near the training manifold, we need to introduce additional symmetry relationships that expand the equivalence class. We restrict our study to discrete distri-butions embedded in the hypercube {− 1, 1}d, noised under continuous Gaussian convolution, and a stochastic localization algorithm to implement the reverse sampling and produce discrete outputs. The benefit of embedding in the hypercube is all discrete points are constant norm ∥x∥2 = d. The conditional densities which define the denoiser are quadratic tilts to the base density which intro-duce linear and quadratic terms to the log-density. By the constant norm property, all quadratic terms normalize out and the denoiser is a pure linear tilt to the base density. This opens up additional symmetries that expand the equivalence class and allow modification to the training manifold. With these additional symmetries, we train a denoiser network that can be evaluated at points which are out-of-distribution from the pure noising of the base distribution, but lie in the equivalence class of points seen at sampling time. We visualize a sketch of this idea in Figure 1. Our contributions are threefold: 1. Conditional generation tasks such as infilling and reward tilting by binary rewards can be cast as linear tilts in an extended state space by promoting the reward coordinate to an additional random variable noised and denoised when sampling. 2Figure 1: Sketch of denoiser symmetry. The training manifold is where the denoiser network has learned the base denoiser. The sampling manifold is the typical vectors that will be encountered when sampling a tilted density. The equivalence relationships can find a point with equal evaluation of the base denoiser near the training manifold, not all equivalence relationships map back to the region of the training manifold. 2. We establish the theory of denoiser symmetry , the evaluation of the denoiser of a linear tilt to a base density can be recast as an evaluation of the base denoiser at a shifted eval-uation point. We establish additional symmetries specific to hypercube embedded discrete distributions, by the removal of quadratic terms in the conditional log-density. 3. We introduce diagonal matrix rescalings to our hypercube embedding during training called Λ focusing , turning an out-of-distribution learning problem into an in-distribution learning problem, and allowing the base denoiser to be evaluated at locations off the typical noising path. We demonstrate performance on small discrete sampling targets such as a non-linear reward tilt to a small 12 dimension Ising model with ground truth comparison denoiser; generating and solving 4x4, 6x6, and 9x9 Sudoku puzzles; and conditional class generation of MNIST digit images. These problems are small enough to act as a test bed where the quality of our sampling can be compared against exact ground truth values as a diagnostic of effective sampling, with future work to focus on scaling and more complex target distributions for internal guidance. 

# 2 Setup 

We consider discrete distributions embedded in the hypercube X → {− 1, 1}d, noised under Gaus-sian convolution, and sampled via a reverse process. This form of sampling was considered in Chen et al. [7], referred to as “analog bits”, although the structural results of linear tilts and internal 3guidance we derive here were not known at the time. Any discrete distribution can be embedded in the hypercube by assigning each element x of the original discrete state space X of size less than 2d, a unique embedding vector e(x) ∈ {− 1, 1}d. This is natural for token generation tasks with a finite library such as language models; constraint satisfaction tasks such as generating and solving Sudoku puzzles; image generation with discrete pixel values such as grayscale MNIST digits; and distributions natively in the hypercube such as Ising models. ## 2.1 Sampling Via Stochastic Localization 

Our sampling algorithm will be the stochastic localization path, which is equivalent to a time shifting and rescaling of the marginal densities of score-based diffusion. See Montanari [23] and our discussion in Appendix B.2 for further connection. The benefit of stochastic localization is the drift term is exactly the denoiser, rather than being related to the score via Tweedie’s formula [25]. This makes it simpler to construct denoiser-to-denoiser relationships rather than score-to-score relationships. 

Definition 2 (Stochastic Localization) . For a target density π, initialize a random variable at the origin X0 = 0. Then follow the SDE using the denoiser as a drift at a specific time scaling, 

dX t = F π(Xt, t, √t)dt + dB t,

then the marginals at time t are equivalent to 

Xt = tX + √tZ, X ∼ π, Z ∼ N (0 , I ).

For large T , the normalized variable XT /T is equal to the target density with a small normal perturbation of scaling 1 /√T . The discrete implementation of this SDE as an algorithm is shown in Algorithm 2.

95 Experiments 

We demonstrate the performance of the method in three experimental settings: a 12-dimensional Ising model with a non-linear reward tilt, 4x4, 6x6, and 9x9 Sudoku unconditional grid generation and puzzle solving (infilling), and MNIST conditional digit generation. Full technical descriptions of the target density, training set, model architecture, and additional details for each experiment can be found in Appendix C. All experiments are sampled up to a stochastic localization time 

T = 20, with the discrete grid {tk}Kk=0 is constructed with K = 1000 and a scheduling algorithm defined in Appendix B.5. We note that at each τ tilt strength, we know the theoretical probability of producing a reward-achieving solution using the log-odds curve as discussed in Appendix B.3. 

## 5.1 12-Dimensional Ising Model 

The 12-dimensional Ising model has a reward tilt which gives reward when the number of positive bits is exactly 10. The state space of the Ising model is small enough to enumerate giving exact ground truth for the denoiser to compare our network against. In Appendix C Figure 8 we see the network denoiser has learned the ground truth denoiser well with low L2 error across tilt strengths, and produces the correct proportion of reward-achieving solutions as predicted by the log-odds curve in Appendix C Figure 9. The Ising model is a small scale experiment with ground truth denoiser to calibrate the method before proceeding to larger state spaces. 

## 5.2 4x4 Sudoku 

The 4x4 Sudoku problem has a small enough state space to enumerate the ground truth denoiser. We compare the network denoiser to the ground truth in L2 error in Appendix C Figure 11, the network learns the denoiser well. For the log-odds curve in Appendix C Figure 12 we can consider the stochastic localization algorithm implemented with either the ground truth denoiser or the network estimation. Both methods track the log-odds up to 98% reward-achieving solutions at 

τ ≈ 3.5, but the network sampler does start to lag at higher tilt strengths while the ground truth denoiser does continue to track at high tilts up to τ = 5. With 288 reward-achieving puzzles, we can track if the network is uniformly sampling these puzzles. In 3,000 generated reward-achieving puzzles, the exact-denoiser sampler visits all 288 solutions, each between 4 and 21 times, and passes a χ2 test of uniformity ( χ2287 = 280 .1, p = 0 .60). The network visits 287 of the 288 solutions, each between 0 and 25 times, and fails the test (χ2287 = 391 .9, p < 10 −4). The network therefore covers nearly the whole solution set, but it does not sample it uniformly: under the reward tilt it has learned to favor some solutions over others. Results are visualized in Appendix C Figure 15. An added benefit of embedding in the hypercube is we can do early bit-by-bit decoding of the continuous vector Xt at a time t based on the coordinate-wise entries of the decoder |E[Xi|Xt]| ≥ 

0.75. One of the main complaints against continuous diffusion samplers for discrete targets relative to autoregressive models and masked diffusion is that because they operate in continuous space there is no discernible discrete token string until the final decoding step. This technique solves that problem and produces a partially complete token string before terminal decoding, visualized in Appendix C Figure 13. 

## 5.3 6x6 Sudoku 

For 6x6 Sudoku, the state space is large and there are vastly more reward-achieving puzzles than the 4x4 problem. In the 4x4 problem, the state space was size 2 32 with only 288 reward-achieving 10 puzzles. The training set of size 150,000 contained 5% reward-achieving puzzles indicated by a 1 in their reward coordinate. Thus each reward-achieving puzzle was seen in the training set and a memorization check was performed with a χ2 test of uniform sampling. For 6x6 Sudoku the state space is 2 108 with 28 million reward-achieving puzzles. The training set of size 5 million contained 5% reward-achieving puzzles, which is 250,000 of the 28 million possible valid grids, 0.88%. We cannot fully enumerate the denoiser for reward tilts in the 6x6 setting, but we can show the 

L2 error for the untilted density τ = 0 is controlled for the learned network in Appendix C Figure 17. The network also properly tracks the log-odds across tilt strengths in Appendix C Figure 18. We check memorization by the rate the network reproduces a reward-achieving puzzle provided in the training set. With the training set containing 0 .88% of the reward-achieving puzzles, in 1,000 reward-achieving generations proper uniform sampling would produce exact matches with the training set by chance 3–14 with a 95% interval. We observe 10 matches in 1,000 samples, consistent with no memorization. 

## 5.4 9x9 Sudoku 

For 9x9 Sudoku the state space is too large to enumerate a ground truth denoiser. The number of reward-achieving puzzles is so large there is a near 0 probability of recreating a training set puzzle under proper sampling. The training set is 10 million Sudoku grids with 5% being reward-achieving puzzles. The 9x9 Sudoku target density is the first example where the effect of self-conditioning is noticeable, self-conditioning is discussed in more detail in Appendix B.4. In summary, we consider two modes of self-conditioning: mode “two-pass” reuses the network’s denoiser evaluation at a given time and maintains a memory one Markov structure. Mode “carry” reuses a previous time point’s denoiser value and breaks this Markov structure but is more commonly used in modern methods. The two methods give different results when tracking the log-odds in Appendix C Figures 20a and 20b. The reward coordinate is denoised jointly with the tokens, so the sampler reports its own prediction of reward in the d + 1 coordinate, and a correctly sampling model should have this prediction agree with the graded reward. The two-pass self-conditioning mode tracks the log-odds in its own reward prediction in its d + 1 coordinate, but not in the graded validity, which plateaus near 60%. The model’s d + 1 coordinate reports it is producing reward-achieving grids far more often than it actually is. The carry self-conditioning mode plateaus near 90% graded validity with a 8–12% gap between predicted and graded reward. The reward function is very sharp, mis-sampling a single bit gives 0 reward. Sampled non-reward grids are typically close to reward ones: 90% of two-pass samples lie within 10 out of 324 bits of the nearest valid grid in Hamming distance (Appendix C Figure 21), consistent with a few bits mis-sampled late in the trajectory being penalized by the sharp validity reward. The model performs both unconditional grid generation and infilling, which some may call “solving” a puzzle, by adding a cell-fixing tilt to the reward tilt (Figure 3). This type of sampling is natural for the diffusion model here which operates under linear tilts and decodes in any order, but would be difficult for a left-to-right autoregressive model which would need to backtrack against later given values. We use a reward tilt strength and infill tilt strength of τ = 3 and carry self-condition mode across various numbers of given cell values in Appendix C Table 1 and Figure 4. Note the sampler may not maintain the initial given cell values as they are a tilt vector not a hard constraint. ## 5.5 MNIST Digit Images 

The MNIST data set consists of 60,000 images of hand drawn digits 0-9 and 10,000 hold out examples. We encode reward as a 10-bit one-hot class block, and generating class tilts along the whole block with 1 class being positively tilted and the other 9 negatively tilted. Generated images are classified by an independent CNN classifier (98 .74% held-out accuracy). Since the scorer has a ceiling to its classifier accuracy, the observable success rate saturates at the classifier’s per-class sensitivity; points beyond τ ≈ 1.69 are uninformative. In Appendix C Figure 29 we see generated pools of images show similar variety and quality as hold out examples not used for training indicating no mode collapse or degradation of digit quality under tilting. In Appendix C Figures 24-27 self-conditioning mode two-pass and carry are also compared for their tracking of the log-odds. In this experiment, carry mode seems to over-sample a class 12 at medium tilts. Two-pass tracks the classifier-corrected exact rate within its confidence interval through τ ≈ 1, in both the classifier score and the network’s own reward block. Carry overshoots the tilt in every class: at τ = 0 .51 it produces the requested class 57 .8% of the time against an exact 45 .9%, with the 95% interval excluding the exact value at every τ from 0 .26 to 1 .03. Both methods produce 95% class correct images at large tilts, and further tilt strengths go beyond the judge CNN’s sensitivity.

## B.2 Stochastic Localization 

Stochastic localization can be viewed as a different time scaling and spatial scaling than the usual score-based diffusion derived from the forwards flow Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process as outlined in Montanari [23]. We re-derive the equivalence here for an audience more familiar with the score-based construction. The score-based diffusion is variance preserving, initialized at a standard normal draw, and uses the score as its drift. The stochastic localization process is not variance preserving (it is growing in magnitude over time), is initialized at a Dirac mass at the origin, and uses the denoiser as its drift term. Both can be derived from the same reverse diffusion process theory. 

Time reversal. Both constructions are reverse-time implementations of a forward diffusion pro-ducing a sequence of marginal densities that are equivalent to convolutions with Gaussian random 22 variables of various mixture amounts. ## B.4 Self-Conditioning 

Self-conditioning as originally introduced in Chen et al. [7] is a method to improve the sample quality of a denoising diffusion model. It was originally derived as a mostly ad hoc engineering 27 decision, rather than a method arising from sound theory. Its interaction with standard theory for error control for diffusion models has not yet been fully understood [30, 18, 6, 5, 3, 9, 34]. In this paper we consider two forms of self-conditioning: one method we call mode “two-pass” is a Markovian form of self-conditioning, it uses only information available at a given time point of a discrete diffusion to estimate the denoiser as in Algorithm 6 in Chen et al. [7]. This method can be considered an architecture choice for the denoiser estimator network and is compatible with existing diffusion model error control theory which views the reverse process as a Euler-Maruyama discretization of the continuous SDE with pointwise denoiser estimation. The second method of self-conditioning we call mode “carry” is when the denoiser reuses its previous estimate at time t − 1 to make its denoiser estimate at time t. This is the more common method implemented in modern denoising diffusion models, however it breaks the memory-one Markov structure under which diffusion model error control theory is currently understood. The denoiser is a conditional expectation on the origin point of a noisy observation given partial information provided by the noised observation,

If we had some additional side information W from some other random variable, this could produce a more precise conditional estimate of the origin point XF (u, w, a ) = Eπ[X|U = aX + √

1 − a2Z, W ].

Target Density 

4 × 4 Sudoku has a state space of size 2 32 , with only 288 valid puzzles which satisfy row, column and box constraints. Each cell is a 2-bit encoding of the numbers 1–4 with no dead code words, so the 16 cells give exactly 32 bits and every bit string decodes to a grid. 

Figure 10: Example valid 4x4 grid. The base distribution is a weak generator sampling each row independently. With probability 1 − ρ it makes the row a uniformly random permutation of the numbers 1–4, and thus a valid row; with probability ρ it instead draws the row’s 8 bits uniformly at random, giving any of the 256 possible rows. As such, if a coordinate ever passes a threshold |E[Xi|Xt]| > ℓ , we can consider that bit “confident” and fix that bit to the sign of the denoiser at that moment. This allows for “early decoding” seeing which bits the network fixes in which order and at what point of the trajectory. Note a bit is allowed to flip signs, all that is decoded is the terminal sign value. Fixed bits determine what tokens (cell values) are still valid among the unfixed bits. One of the arguments against continuous diffusion for discrete targets is that the trajectory remains a continuous value that cannot be interpreted in discrete token space until the terminal decoding step, unlike masked diffusion or autoregressive models which build partial discrete token strings along their trajectory. By using the confidence based bit decoding, we can achieve a similar effect to see what discrete set of tokens the puzzle was considering at each moment in the trajectory and soft decode at any time. When both bits in a cell are fixed we record the cell value, when one bit is fixed such as ( ∗, −1) this means the cell will get either a (1 , −1) which is token 2 or ( −1 − 1) which is token 1. We record this with Sudoku “pencil marks” showing what is considered still possible in the cell. We note the network is not “solving” the puzzle “logically” as a human would by applying row, column, and box constraints in sequence. This would rule out all 1’s in that column, but the upper box cells in the same column have not yet ruled out a 1 in that column since the bits for those two cells have not yet reached their own confidence level. This is therefore a sequence of most likely puzzles tracking the most confident bits at each moment in the trajectory. Model Architecture 

The target distributions and models were trained in order from smallest to largest state space starting with the Ising Model, then 4x4, 6x6, 9x9 Sudoku, then the MNIST problem. As the state 40 space scaled up in size, larger data sets were needed to learn the more complicated density and tilt, and more expressive model architectures. The previous Ising and 4x4 Sudoku problems used MLP models with no attention mechanism. For 6x6, 9x9 Sudoku and MNIST we use a transformer architecture with attention. This problem uses a transformer featurizer rather than the residual MLP, since the row, column and box constraints are joint combinatorial structure that a flat trunk must discover entirely from data, whereas attention can relate cells directly. The 108 core bits are split into 6 tokens of 18 bits (one per row), and the reward coordinate is given its own token rather than folded into the level conditioning, so attention can relate x∗ to every row and vice versa; attention is non-causal over the resulting 7 slots. The model has 4 layers with dmodel = 256, 8 attention heads, and feed-forward dimension 1024, and noise-level conditioning enters by AdaLN from the same Fourier level embedding e(a) used elsewhere. The output head is unchanged, preserving the mean-field skip 

Gθ(u, a ) = tanh (β(a) u + gθ(a) ⊙ rθ(u, a )),

with every residual branch zero-initialized so that at initialization the network is exactly the mean-field denoiser. The network is additionally self-conditioned, as discussed in Appendix B.4, the previous estimate of x0 is concatenated to the input along the feature axis of each token, doubling the per-slot input channels from 18 to 36. The model has 3 ,249 ,028 parameters, substantially fewer than the 4 × 4 MLP despite the much larger state space. Full implementation details are in the supplementary code.

## C.5 MNIST Conditional Image Generation 

Target Density 

MNIST consists of 28 × 28 grayscale images of handwritten digits 0–9, with pixel intensities in 

{0, . . . , 255 }. Each pixel is encoded with 8 bits, giving 784 × 8 = 6 ,272 core bits and a state space of 2 6272 . Since the pixel values have a natural ordering, we use a Gray code encoding for each pixel, so that adjacent intensity levels differ in exactly one bit. Since there are 10 digit classes, the reward is a 10-bit block rather than a single coordinate. The class label c is stored as a one-hot pattern rc ∈ {− 1, 1}10 , +1 in position c and −1 elsewhere, appended to the image bits to give the extended state space {− 1, 1}6282 . To generate class c we tilt along the whole pattern, h = τ r c, rather than along the single axis ec. Thus one class receives a positive tilt while the others all receive a negative one. This causes the log-odds of a class to have a slope of 4, logit πτ (c) = logit π(c) + 4 τ, 

where π(c) is the training-set frequency of class c (0 .090–0 .112). The slope is 4 rather than the slope 2 of the single-bit reward tilt in the Sudoku and Ising problems. 

Training Set 

The training set is the standard 60 ,000 MNIST training images and their labels, each encoded as above. The official 10 ,000-image test split is held out and never used for training. It is used for three things: (i) a fixed batch of 4 ,096 held-out images, noised with the training recipe, is scored every 1 ,000 steps to monitor overfitting; (ii) it provides the real-digit comparison in Figure 28; and (iii) it is used to measure the error rates of the classifier that scores generated samples (see Extended Results). 

Model Architecture 

We use the same self-conditioned transformer as for 6 × 6 and 9 × 9 Sudoku, with tokens built from image patches. The image is divided into a 7 × 7 grid of non-overlapping 4 × 4 pixel patches. 48 Each patch becomes one token of 16 × 8 = 128 bits (pixels in row order within a patch, Gray bits most significant first), giving 49 image tokens. The 10-bit class block is given its own token, and non-causal attention runs over the resulting 50 slots. The model has 4 layers with dmodel = 256, 8 attention heads, and feed-forward dimension 1024, with AdaLN noise-level conditioning from the Fourier level embedding e(a) and the same zero-initialized mean-field skip 

Gθ(u, a ) = tanh (β(a) u + gθ(a) ⊙ rθ(u, a )).

Self-conditioning concatenates the previous x0 estimate to each token’s features, doubling the per-slot input channels from 128 to 256. The model has 3 ,555 ,256 parameters. Full implementation details are in the supplementary code. 

Training Run 

The model was trained for 30 ,000 Adam steps with batch size 2048, learning rate 10 −3, and a cosine-annealing schedule, with the self-conditioning input supplied with probability 0 .5. Signal levels are drawn log-uniformly on β ∈ [0 .02 , 50]. Λ focusing is applied to the whole 10-bit reward block, maintaining the sign of those inputs but increasing their magnitude. The λ is 1 with probability 0 .25 and otherwise log-uniform on [1 , 24]. The upper limit differs from the Sudoku runs: because the whole 10-coordinate block is stretched at once, the admissible range is λ < √d/ 10 = 25 .06 rather than √d, and we train just below this limit. 30 ,000 ×2048 ≈ 6.1×10 7 draws amount to roughly 1 ,000 passes over the 60 ,000 training images. The held-out loss decreased steadily from 720 .6 at step 1 ,000 to 632 .1 at step 30 ,000 and was still flat-to-decreasing at the end. This gives no sign of overfitting. Training ran on a single NVIDIA H100 80GB GPU on the PSC Bridges-2 cluster for 1 hour and 15 minutes. Processing 6 .1 × 10 7 samples through a 3 ,555 ,256-parameter network corresponds to approximately 1 .3 × 10 15 training FLOPs under the standard 6 N D estimate. 

Extended Results 

To detect if a generated image is of a given class, we train an independent score CNN classifier. The scorer is a two-layer CNN (1.2M parameters) trained for 3 epochs with Adam on the 60,000 MNIST training images, reaching 98.74% accuracy on the held-out test split. The classifier is an imperfect instrument and cannot detect very strong tilt accuracies beyond its own classification error. Its error is also not constant across classes. As such, our ability to detect tilt strength saturates at the classifier accuracy. Near the top of the tilt range its error is the dominant source of uncertainty. Let sc be the probability the classifier labels a true class-c image as c, and ec the probability it labels an image of another class as c. For a true class-c rate p, the rate we observe is 

m = p s c + (1 − p) ec.

We measure sc and ec on the held-out set ( sc ranges from 0 .962 for class 9 to 0 .997 for class 1; 

ec ≤ 0.0023). We then compare the observed rate with the exact marginal passed through this equation, rather than correcting the observed rate. The observed rate therefore levels off at about 

sc rather than 1 (log-odds 3 .2–5 .9 depending on the class). Once the corrected exact rate comes within two standard errors of this limit, which happens for τ ≥ 1.69 in every class, the sample size is not what limits resolution; the classifier cannot distinguish further improvements. The self-conditioning modes two-pass (Markovian) and carry (non-Markovian) behave in the opposite way to 9 × 9 Sudoku. The carry model over-samples the reward class at a given tilt, producing more outputs of a given class than the log-odds would predict. The two-pass mode tracks the log-odds more truthfully. With two-pass , the classifier-scored rate matches the corrected exact rate within its confidence interval, or nearly so, from τ = 0 through τ ≈ 1 (e.g. 1 .78 vs. 1 .82 at τ = 1 .03), and the network’s reward block follows the same curve. With carry , the sampler overshoots over the whole rising region. At τ = 0 .51 it produces the requested class 57 .8% of the time against an exact rate of 45 .9% (log-odds 0 .31 vs. −0.16), and the 95% interval excludes the exact value at every τ from 0 .26 to 1.03.

# ABSTRACT 

Autoregressive (AR) video generation makes long-horizon visual synthesis possible, but every generated chunk requires repeated forward passes through a large diffusion transformer (DiT). Training-free caching reduces this cost by reusing intermediate computation. However, existing cache controllers often couple two decisions: when the cache is synchronized exactly and how much 

approximate computation is spent in between. Our characterization suggests that these decisions should be separated. For reactive staleness signals, exact synchronization should be scheduled before measured drift accumulates, while measured drift should decide only how much repair to spend between synchronizations. Based on this separation, we introduce Frequency-Step Cache (FSC) , a training-free controller that performs exact synchronization at full-computation anchors on a dense-to-sparse schedule and adaptive repair between them. A lightweight latent-space risk combining frequency-resolved drift with token age routes each non-anchor step from whole-step replay to selective token repair or full repair without changing the synchronization schedule. We further show on SkyReels-V2 that whole-step reuse provides the main acceleration, while selective token computation is most effective as fidelity repair. In a controlled ablation, using one reactive signal for both synchronization and repair reduces fidelity at matched latency, with the separation advantage also observed on FramePack and Matrix-Game. Across multiple AR video backbones, FSC provides favorable speed–quality trade-offs. On SkyReels-V2, FSC-Speed reaches a 2.36 ×

# 1 Introduction 

Autoregressive (AR) video generation has emerged as a practical paradigm for long-horizon visual synthesis, generating videos sequentially by conditioning each new chunk on previously generated content (Chen et al., 2024a; Yin et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Sand.ai et al., 2025). This chunk-wise formulation makes long-video generation possible while keeping attention context and memory use bounded. However, each chunk still requires tens of denoising steps, which refine a noisy video representation through repeated evaluations of a large diffusion transformer (Peebles and Xie, 2023). Training-free caching reduces this cost by reusing intermediate outputs across nearby steps (Ma et al., 2024; Selvaraju et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025a). Yet redundancy varies across steps and video content, making it difficult to decide when reuse remains accurate (Zou et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025b). Reuse introduces approximation errors that can accumulate across steps and chunks (Cui et al., 2026a; Peng et al., 2026; Nan et al., 2026). Existing cache controllers often use reactive reuse signals to govern both when exact computation occurs and how much approximation is allowed in between. These two roles operate differently: synchronization establishes the reference state for subsequent reuse, whereas repair responds to staleness observed after reuse. Cache control therefore involves two distinct decisions. (1) When should the cache be fully refreshed? We call this exact synchronization :full computation updates cached outputs and establishes a reference for measuring subsequent changes in the video representation. (2) How much should be recomputed between refreshes? We call this adaptive repair : choosing how much computation to perform based on observed changes that indicate inaccurate reuse. Our characterization motivates separating these decisions (Sec. 3). Reuse accuracy varies strongly by step, favoring scheduled synchronization (Fig. 1(a)). Approximate synchronization leaves errors in the cached outputs reused by later steps, while change-based measurements report deviations only after they occur (Fig. 1(d)). We therefore schedule exact synchronization independently of these measurements and use them only to allocate repair. In a controlled ablation, a purely reactive variant has lower fidelity at matched latency on all eight development prompts (Sec. 5.3). Between synchronizations, repair must balance accuracy against execution cost. On SkyReels-V2, selectively computing video tokens, the model’s units of video representation, yields much less speedup than the computation reduction suggests (Fig. 1(c)). This favors reusing an entire step’s cached outputs for acceleration and selective computation for repair. To guide repair, a lightweight score based on changes in the video representation tracks reuse error. The score reaches a Spearman rank correlation of 0.893 with reuse error measured against full computation, as shown in Fig. 3. Guided by these findings, Frequency-Step Cache (FSC) schedules full-computation steps, called anchors , progressively farther apart (Sec. 4.1). Between anchors, it reuses cached outputs for a whole step or repairs them by recomputing varying numbers of tokens or performing full computation (Sec. 4.3). The repair score combines low- and high-frequency changes, representing coarse structure and fine detail respectively, with token age, the steps since each token’s last update (Sec. 4.2). These measurements control repair, never the anchor schedule. Our contributions are: (i) separating scheduled synchronization from measurement-driven repair, outperforming coupled control at approximately matched latency on SkyReels-V2, FramePack, and Matrix-Game; (ii) showing on SkyReels-V2 that whole-step reuse supplies acceleration while selective token computation is most effective as fidelity repair; and (iii) FSC, a training-free controller that combines scheduled exact anchors with lightweight frequency- and age-aware adaptive repair. Experiments across multiple AR video backbones demonstrate favorable speed–quality trade-offs and consistently support the separation principle. On SkyReels-V2, FSC-Speed reaches a 2.36 × speedup, above MotionCache’s 

2.275 ×, while improving same-seed SSIM by 0.019 and PSNR by 0.5 dB, with comparable VBench quality. At MotionCache-matched speed, FSC-Quality improves SSIM by 0.031 , PSNR by 1.0 dB, and lowers LPIPS by 0.024 .

# 2 Related Work 

Autoregressive Video Generation. Recent AR video models generate long videos as sequences of causally con-ditioned frames or chunks with diffusion backbones (Chen et al., 2024a; Yin et al., 2025), often trained with flow matching (Chen et al., 2025; Sand.ai et al., 2025). This chunk-wise formulation enables long-horizon generation under bounded context and memory, but each chunk still requires repeated denoising computation. 

Training-Free Diffusion Caching. Feature caching reduces repeated denoising computation by reusing activations or residuals across nearby steps, using fixed schedules (FORA (Selvaraju et al., 2024), ∆-DiT (Chen et al., 2024b)), adaptive timing (TeaCache (Liu et al., 2025a), DiCache (Bu et al., 2026)), or forecasting (TaylorSeer (Liu et al., 2Frequency-Step Cache A P REPRINT 

2025c)). Token-wise methods instead combine reuse with selective recomputation: ToCa (Zou et al., 2025) uses a predetermined refresh cycle with attention- and age-based token ranking, DuCa (Zou et al., 2026) alternates reuse and token recomputation, and ProCache (Cao et al., 2026) uses an offline-searched reuse schedule. ToCa studies token-wise caching in diffusion transformers but not chunk-autoregressive long-video cache control. It also fixes both the refresh cycle and token-recomputation budget a priori; attention and cache age rank which tokens receive that budget, whereas FSC adapts the repair amount online while keeping synchronization scheduled. 

Caching for Autoregressive Video. FlowCache (Ma et al., 2026) assigns chunk-level reuse policies, MotionCache (Xu et al., 2026) introduces motion-aware token adaptivity, and ARCache (Nan et al., 2026) determines whole-step reuse from history-token changes. SCOPE (Cui et al., 2026b) expands the inter-step action space through prediction and selective frame computation. These methods adapt reuse to temporal redundancy, but do not explicitly formulate exact-synchronization timing and intermediate repair amount as separate control decisions. 

Frequency-Aware Cache Control. Frequency-aware methods use spectral information to schedule whole-step refreshes (SeaCache (Chung et al., 2026)) or manipulate frequency components (FreqCa (Liu et al., 2025b), Spec-tralCache (Li, 2026)). PreciseCache (Wang et al., 2026a) uses low-frequency differences to decide when denoising computation can be skipped. FSC instead keeps exact synchronization timing independent of measured staleness: synchronization is scheduled, while frequency-resolved drift and cache age determine only inter-anchor repair. 

# 3 Characterization and Observations 

We first characterize where redundancy exists and where naive caching fails in controlled profiling experiments (App. A.1). The resulting observations motivate the design; full-scale autoregressive ablations of the deployed controller (Sec. 5) reproduce the relevant orderings. 

Observation 1: Cacheability is strongly step-dependent. Reusing the previous step’s residuals at early steps incurs approximately 2.4× the error of reuse at late steps (Fig. 1(a)). Under a comparable anchor budget, dense-to-sparse placement yields the highest mean SSIM (Fig. 1(b); Sec. 5.3). Design consequence: anchors should be dense early and increasingly sparse later (Sec. 4.1). 

Observation 2: Sparse token computation gives limited speedup on SkyReels-V2. Fig. 1(c) profiles true sparse kernels, which recompute a subset of tokens per step: at q=0 .10 they reach 1.94 × against a compute-proportional 

2.72 ×, mainly because full-context key/value handling and launch overhead do not shrink with token fraction. Their stale keys and values also cost ≈ 0.024 SSIM relative to a dense-forward token-refresh quality reference (its ≈ 1.23 × is fixed by the full forward; App. A.3). Whole-step dense-to-sparse reuse reaches 2.62 ×. FSC’s deployed token repair is a gathered-batch implementation whose cost is measured end to end. Design consequence: on this backbone, whole-step reuse should supply the acceleration, and token computation should primarily serve as fidelity repair (Sec. 4.3). 

Observation 3: Approximate anchors seldom improve upon exact anchors. Only three of the 17 tested variants lie above the exact-anchor frontier (Fig. 1(d); App. A.4). Any approximation inside an anchor is inherited by every step until the next anchor. Design consequence: anchors should remain exact full computations (Sec. 4.1). 

Observation 4: Inter-anchor staleness is spatially heterogeneous. Between anchors, tokens go stale at different rates. High-frequency reuse error grows faster in absolute terms than low-frequency (LF) error. It remains about 2–2.5×

the LF error at cache ages 1–10, up to 2.7× on individual prompts (Fig. 5 in App. A.5). Cache age complements drift: a token with small per-step drift can still become unreliable after many reuse steps. Design consequence: repair between anchors should follow measured staleness, proxied by frequency-resolved drift and cache age (Sec. 4.2). 

Summary: Because reactive signals report drift only after it occurs, these observations motivate the following control principle: schedule exact synchronization in advance (Obs. 1, 3), while using measured drift only to allocate inter-anchor repair (Obs. 2, 4).

On MAGI-1, FSC scales the native token-level reuse threshold, forces exact computation at anchors, and fully recomputes every ktok -th step (App. B.5). MAGI-1 therefore tests transfer through its native cache interface, not the frequency controller. The controller needs no auxiliary network and adds under 0.2% latency (App. B.6).

5.1 Experimental Setup Base Models and Baselines. SkyReels-V2, FramePack (Zhang et al., 2025), and Matrix-Game-3.0 (Wang et al., 2026b) use the full FSC controller, whereas MAGI-1 tests transfer through its native cache interface (Sec. 4.4). SkyReels-V2-1.3B (Chen et al., 2025) generates 540P videos of 177 frames (two 97 -frame chunks with 17 overlapping frames, 50 steps per temporal group; App. D). MAGI-1-4.5B-distill (Sand.ai et al., 2025) generates 720 × 720 videos of 168 frames (seven 24 -frame chunks, 64 steps per chunk). We compare against vanilla, FlowCache (Ma et al., 2026) with chunk-level reuse, MotionCache (Xu et al., 2026) with token-level reuse in its released configuration, and our latency-calibrated SeaCache port. All share one stack, prompts, seed, checkpoints, and settings; SCOPE (Cui et al., 2026b) runs on its official stack. FlowCache runs with its KV-cache compression disabled. In a probe run, the official FlowCache stack without compression runs at 2.21 ×, close to our reproduction ( 2.04 ×), at comparable fidelity (App. E). We found no public implementation of ARCache (Nan et al., 2026). 

Operating Points. We report two operating points of the same controller. On SkyReels-V2, FSC-Quality matches MotionCache’s latency and is the single configuration used for all SkyReels-V2 ablations, sensitivity and seed studies, and the 14B no-retuning transfer; FSC-Speed differs only in warmup length and threshold scale and is a faster point on the speed–quality frontier (App. E.1). On MAGI-1, both points are calibrated on its native cache interface and run faster than MotionCache. FramePack and Matrix-Game each use one per-backbone calibrated configuration (App. E.2).

5.3 Ablation Studies Separating Synchronization from Repair. Removing the anchors and letting ρs trigger both repair and full synchro-nization gives a controlled purely reactive variant of FSC: it uses the same risk signal and repair machinery but has no scheduled anchors. At matched latency, FSC-Quality improves SSIM by 0.131 and lowers LPIPS by 0.186 on all eight development prompts. # 6 Conclusion 

Our results support one principle for cache control in AR video diffusion: for reactive staleness signals, exact synchronization should be scheduled before measured drift accumulates, and measured drift should only decide how much repair to spend between synchronizations. FSC instantiates this principle through scheduled exact anchors and whole-step replay, with a frequency- and age-aware latent risk controlling inter-anchor repair. On SkyReels-V2 (1.3B and, unchanged, 14B), FSC is closer to the vanilla trajectory than MotionCache in SSIM and PSNR at equal or higher speed, and coupling both decisions into one risk signal reduces fidelity at matched latency (Sec. 5.3). On MAGI-1, FSC’s native-interface port improves speed and fidelity over MotionCache at comparable VBench, and the separation advantage holds on FramePack and Matrix-Game with per-backbone calibration.

B.6 Complexity 

Per denoising step, the SkyReels controller performs one 2 × 2 spatial average pool, an upsampling and subtraction to obtain the two bands, and per-token norm and ranking operations. The latent band operations are linear in the latent size, while percentile computation and token ranking add an O(N log N ) per-group term in the token count N . No auxiliary network or additional transformer pass is required. A GPU micro-benchmark with the deployed shapes ( 10 ,200 tokens per group; 612 routing decisions per video, both guidance phases, weighted by the measured routing shares, plus one band extraction per forward) gives about 0.23 s per video, under 0.2% of the 161 s generation latency; band extraction alone is below 0.05% . The measured acceleration comes from skipping transformer residual computation through whole-step replay and selective token reuse.

FLOPs Accounting. On SkyReels-V2 the executed-token trace of every transformer block is recorded for all 946 

prompts after the token-cache warmup; the untraced warmup and overlap-prefix forwards are full computations and are added for every method, vanilla included (five full forwards per temporal group for FlowCache and MotionCache, i.e., their warmup 4 plus the cache-initializing step that follows it; two for FSC and the SeaCache port). PFLOPs follow from the per-token cost of the block’s linear layers and self-attention over the group’s own tokens, with the unconditional pass charged as the conditional one; attention to cached context keys and cross-attention are not charged, so SkyReels-V2 PFLOPs are comparable across methods but are not a complete count and are not on the same basis as the MAGI-1 values. The MAGI-1 runtime exposes no such trace, so we instrument its block stack to count executed token-slots and attended query–key pairs per forward and compare them against the vanilla schedule replayed for the same step. The analytic vanilla cost is 140 .6 PFLOPs per video ( 12 ,150 tokens per chunk, seven chunks, 64 

steps, a four-chunk denoising window with the model’s noise2clean_kvrange ; linear layers 55 .9, self-attention 84 .2,cross-attention with caption projections 0.5 PFLOPs), 1.2% above the 139 PFLOPs reported by MotionCache for the identical setting, and the instrumented counter reproduces it exactly ( 1.000 ) on a vanilla run. Each cached method’s PFLOPs is this vanilla cost times its measured executed fraction: FlowCache on the full run, MotionCache and both FSC points on the same instrumented 20 -prompt sample (both FSC points were instrumented separately with the same counter; FSC-Quality’s per-prompt latencies reproduce its benchmark run within 1% ). Sparse forwards are charged for executed query tokens only, matching the SkyReels-V2 convention; charging the full reassembled key/value length instead changes the MAGI-1 fractions by less than 0.03 .

Timing Procedure. Latency is wall-clock time of the complete generation call, bracketed by 

torch.cuda.synchronize() , measured per prompt. Speedup is computed per prompt as the same-prompt vanilla latency divided by the method latency on the same GPU model, then averaged over prompts; vanilla latencies are measured once per prompt and cached. Because latency is sensitive to co-located jobs on shared servers, all timed runs are launched on otherwise-idle GPUs, and a post-hoc audit flags any run whose latency leaves the per-configuration clean band (the fastest-quintile latency +2% ; on idle GPUs the latency of a fixed workload varies by less than 1% ). The flagged SkyReels-V2 runs ( 348 of the 946 runs of the W =5 , r=1 .65 operating point of Table 6, generated while other jobs shared its GPUs, and one FSC-Speed run) were re-measured on idle GPUs, after which every vanilla, FlowCache, and FSC run lies inside its band and all speedups of Table 6 average over all 946 prompts. MotionCache’s and SeaCache-AR’s executed compute varies more across prompts, which places 95 and 530 of their runs above this band; measured instead against the latency predicted from each run’s executed compute, no MotionCache run and five SeaCache-AR runs are more than 2% slower, and excluding those five would change SeaCache-AR’s speedup by

Schedule search. We next evaluate whether a local surrogate can recover the schedule.

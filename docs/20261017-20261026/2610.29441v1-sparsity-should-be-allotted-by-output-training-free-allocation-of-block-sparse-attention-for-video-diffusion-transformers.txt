## Abstract 

Full 3D attention is costly in video Diffusion Transformers. Block-sparse attention reduces this cost by computing only selected attention blocks. However, existing methods either overlook the potential to optimize sparsity allocation or rely on heuristic rules or local metrics to guide it. In this work, we observe that sparsifying attention in different layers and at different denoising steps affects final-video fidelity to sub-stantially different degrees. Yet the identities of the most sensitive layer–step pairs remain nearly unchanged across inputs. Building on these findings, we propose Allocating 

Layer–step Latency by Output Tests ( ALLOT ), a training-free sparsity allocation method. Treating each layer–step pair as a cell, ALLOT estimates each cell’s contribution to final-video error through (i) cell probing and (ii) trajec-tory calibration, then jointly optimizes cell densities under a measured-latency budget. It preserves existing selection strategies and kernels, replacing only the allocation compo-nent. At equal or shorter wall-clock time, ALLOT outper-forms every compared allocation strategy with XAttention, with an 11.8 dB PSNR gain on HunyuanVideo. Extensive experiments across four additional published pipelines on Wan2.1, Wan2.2, and HunyuanVideo show that ALLOT out-performs or ties the default in all nine settings. 

## 1. Introduction 

Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) [17] have been widely adopted for video generation. Models such as Wan [19] and HunyuanVideo [11] produce high-quality, spatio-temporally consistent videos, yet the cost of their full 3D attention grows quadratically with the number of tokens [18]. On a single GPU, Wan2.1-14B takes roughly one hour to generate a 720p, 81-frame video, and attention accounts for more than half of the end-to-end time. Sparse attention has been widely studied as a means of reducing this computational cost, with existing approaches broadly categorized as either training-free or training-based.  

> *Work done during an internship at KlingAI. †Corresponding author.

Many training-free methods [14, 24, 25, 29] adopt block-sparse attention , which partitions query and key tokens into blocks. These methods estimate the importance of each query–key block pair and compute dense attention only for selected pairs. These works primarily address two questions: which blocks participate in the attention computation, and how to execute the resulting sparse computation efficiently at the kernel level. Notably, beyond determining which blocks participate in the computation, the number of blocks retained in each layer at each denoising step also significantly affects generation quality and latency. In threshold-based methods [14, 24, 29], the thresholds serve as an implicit allocation rule. With all other conditions identical, changing only the allocation can substantially alter final-video fidelity. Despite the existence of some explicit allocation approaches, existing methods generally have the following two limitations: • Heuristic rules do not transfer reliably across settings. 

Many works use fixed rules, such as keeping the first layer or the first 10–30% of denoising steps dense [12, 14, 25]. Our measurements in §3 show that effective allocations vary across models and selection strategies. Consequently, a rule tuned for one setting does not reliably transfer to another and must be re-tuned. • Local metrics do not capture final-video effects. Auto-matic allocation methods commonly rely on local proxies, such as cumulative attention mass [14, 24], Hellinger dis-tance [36], and one-step output error [3, 35], rather than direct measurements of final-video error. Our analysis in §3 shows that the rankings induced by these proxies dis-agree with those based on final-video error, and allocations guided by these metrics can even yield lower final-video fidelity than a uniform baseline. To address these limitations, we term each (layer, step) pair a cell and evaluate it directly by its sensitivity , defined as the degradation it induces in the final video relative to the dense output. ## 2. Related Work 

Block-sparse attention. Block-sparse attention methods for video DiTs are either trained or training-free. Trained methods (VSA [30], VMoBA [21], NABLA [15]) train or fine-tune the model with sparse attention. Training-free methods either apply static masks [12, 31, 36] or select blocks at run time [14, 22–25, 29]. ALLOT retains the se-lection strategies and kernels of existing methods, replacing only the allocation (§4.5). 

Allocation in existing methods. Existing methods allo-cate density, a threshold or a sparse pattern per layer [3], per (layer, head) [1, 14, 35], per step [9, 33], per (layer, step) [28], or per (step, layer, head) [27, 36], based on atten-tion mass, Hellinger distance, one-step output error or other local metrics. Across steps, most use a heuristic warm-up or a preset shape, or treat all steps equally. ALLOT also allocates per (layer, step) but calibrates each cell against the final video to capture cross-step error amplification and solves for all cells jointly under a measured-latency budget. 

## 3. Motivation and Key Observations 

Setup. Let ℓ index layers and s index denoising steps. The sensitivity S(ℓ, s ; b) of cell (ℓ, s ) measures how much spar-sifying that cell to density b degrades the final video relative to the dense reference. Measuring this directly for every cell would require thousands of full generations and is therefore impractical. We instead decompose it into two cheaper sig-nals: the one-step output error when one layer is sparsified and the final-video error when a window of steps is sparsified (§4.2, §4.3). 

Observation 1: Layer sensitivity varies widely and is not reliably reflected by attention-map metrics. As shown in Figure 2(a), the first one or two layers have one-step errors up to 154 times the median-layer error. When evaluated on the final video, the most sensitive layers coincide with these high-error layers. Attention-map metrics (attention mass [14] and Hellinger distance [36]) yield different sensitivity rankings, and allocations based on them degrade fidelity (Appendix B). Cell probes therefore provide a more reliable basis for layer allocation. Observation 2: Final-video effects vary sharply across denoising steps and are understated by one-step er-rors. As shown in Figure 2(b, c), earlier windows produce markedly larger final-video errors than later ones under the same sparsification configuration. The first-to-last-window MSE ratio reaches several hundred for some models. By contrast, the corresponding one-step errors show a much smaller difference and even reverse the ordering of the first and last windows on Wan2.2. One-step errors therefore require trajectory calibration before guiding per-step density. 

Observation 3: Sensitivity rankings remain stable across inputs for a fixed model and selection strategy. Across all tested models, per-input sensitivity rankings closely match rankings aggregated over calibration inputs, with me-dian Spearman correlations of 0.80–1.00 (Appendix D). Prior work reports similar input stability for attention-map met-rics [14, 36]. Our results extend this observation to output-error sensitivity, allowing cell probes and trajectory gains to be measured offline and reused across prompts. 

## 4. ALLOT 

ALLOT jointly optimizes the density of every (layer, step) cell under a measured-latency budget, minimizing the esti-mated total sensitivity. It consists of three modules: cell probing (§4.2), trajectory calibration (§4.3) and latency-constrained allocation (§4.4), built on the first-order analysis and calibrated estimate of §4.1. §4.5 describes inference and integration with existing pipelines.

4.2. Cell probing on the model output 

The cell probe ˆeℓ(t, b ) measures a layer’s output error within one step. It is used to compare layers within the same step (Observation 1) and to compare one cell’s error across den-sities. We add noise at level t to a small number of real videos, sparsify only layer ℓ to density b while keeping all other layers dense, and measure the MSE between this step’s velocity prediction and the dense model’s prediction [3]. At deployment, all other layers are sparse at the same time, un-like the measurement condition. This difference is analyzed in Appendix F. Each measurement needs only one forward pass. By Ob-servation 3, layer rankings are input-stable, so a few probing videos suffice (§5.6). Since rankings differ across selection strategies, probes are by default measured under the selec-tion strategy used at deployment. We tabulate probes on a grid over density and noise level, average over the probing videos, and interpolate in log b–log ˆ e when solving (grid in §5.1).

4.4. Latency-constrained allocation 

Latency table. r(b) is the time of one attention call at density b divided by that of dense FlashAttention [2] on the same shape, measured with the kernel and resolution used at deployment. r(b) is not proportional to density. A sparse kernel is faster than dense only below a crossover density. Assuming cost proportional to density or FLOPs would therefore overestimate the gain from sparsity. When solving, r is clipped to 1, and cells whose density would lie above the crossover are set to dense. 

Solver. Equation (4) is solved by Lagrangian relaxation [3]. The candidate density set B is a set of equally spaced sparse densities plus dense ( b=1 ). Cell probes are interpolated to B

and the step’s noise level, then multiplied by w(s) to obtain 

ˆS(ℓ, s ; b) for each candidate. The solver runs a bisection on λ. Given λ, each cell independently picks the density minimizing ˆS(ℓ, s ; b) + λ r (b) over B, yielding a density table. If the table’s total cost exceeds C, λ is increased. Otherwise, it is decreased. This process continues until convergence (Algorithm 1, Appendix F).

(4) (restricted to B)when the budget equals the table’s own total cost [6]. The result applies to the discrete estimated objective and requires no convexity (Appendix E). 

The bisection locates the two Lagrangian tables bracketing C,which typically differ in only one cell (Appendix E). Warm-up length, which layers stay dense and how density varies across steps all emerge from the solver without heuristic rules (Appendices F and J). 

4.5. Inference and integration with existing selec-tion strategies 

Inference. The density table is solved offline, once per model, selection strategy and latency budget. At generation time, the density of each layer is set from the table before each step s. Cells with bℓs =1 use the original dense attention. All other cells are sparsified by the selection strategy at density bℓs and computed by the original kernel. The same table is used for all prompts. Changing the budget only requires re-solving one table. 

Integration. ALLOT keeps each selection strategy’s ker-nel and ranking, replacing only the cutoff that determines how many ranked blocks or clusters are retained. Threshold-based defaults (SVOO, SVG2, SpargeAttn and XAttention) are converted to a density cutoff. For SpargeAttn, this uses its official top-k API. This removes native per-head or per-input adaptation. Splitting by SVOO’s head densities is 1.44 dB worse than an equal split (Appendices B and F). Throughout, “changing only the allocation” refers to this integration. 

Supported interfaces. ALLOT applies when a selection strategy supports a controllable per-cell density and its la-tency can be measured. Fixed-mask strategies such as Radial are restricted to densities reachable by their mask parame-ters. Density denotes retained blocks for mean-pooled top-k,SpargeAttn, Radial and XAttention, and retained key tokens for the variable-size clusters of SVOO and SVG2. 

## 5. Experiments 

5.1. Experimental setup 

Models and data. Four models: Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B and 14B (hereafter 1.3B and 14B), Wan2.2-T2V-A14B [19] and HunyuanVideo [11]. Resolution and frame count are given in each table, following a published setting where one exists. The analyses in §3 use 480p ×33. All use 50 denoising steps. Wan2.2 results are in Tabs. 2 and 3, and HunyuanVideo results are in Tab. 2 and Appendices G and J. The evaluation set consists of 32 VBench [10] prompts. 

Metrics. Fidelity metrics (PSNR, SSIM [20] and LPIPS [32]) measure similarity to the dense reference (same prompt and seed) rather than absolute generation quality. Perceptual quality is measured by VBench [10], weighted over the six dimensions used by HEART [35]. At matched or shorter wall-clock time, a win requires a positive lower bound of the paired 95% PSNR-difference interval and lower mean LPIPS, while a loss requires a negative upper bound and higher mean LPIPS. For mean-pooled top-k, the uniform baseline keeps the first 15 steps dense. The strengthened baseline addition-ally keeps layer 0 dense and tunes the warm-up on 1.3B (Appendix J).

5.2. Comparison with other allocation methods 

To compare allocation strategies in isolation, we fix XAt-tention’s selection strategy and kernel, changing only the allocation (Tab. 1). ALLOT outperforms every compared allocation method, all of which use hand-set rules or local metrics, by 1.53–7.31 dB. The effect of density truncation depends on the selection strategy: it lowers SVOO’s PSNR but improves XAttention’s. ALLOT further improves on XAttention’s density-cutoff default at matched wall-clock time (Appendix F). VBench scores remain close to those of the baselines. ALLOT also outperforms every compared allocation method on additional XAttention settings spanning higher resolution and HunyuanVideo, all at equal or shorter wall-clock time (Appendix G). 

5.3. Integration with published pipelines 

We integrate ALLOT into the official pipelines of SVOO [14], SVG2 [25], SpargeAttn [29] and Radial [12], changing only the allocation (Tab. 2, details in Appendix H). ALLOT outperforms the default in six of the nine settings (by up to 5.03 dB in PSNR) and ties in the other three (Ap-pendix H). Notably, on HunyuanVideo (13B) with SVOO, ALLOT improves PSNR by 3.38 dB while increasing end-to-end speedup from 1.75 × to 1.90 ×. Figure 4 shows quali-tative comparisons. To compare across speeds, we solve ALLOT’s tables at several latency budgets on three published pipelines (1.3B 720p) and plot PSNR against end-to-end speedup (Figure 5). All of the pipelines’ own settings (open circles) lie below ALLOT’s curve. 

5.4. Cross-model comparison 

On the three Wan models with mean-pooled top-k, ALLOT outperforms both the uniform and the strengthened baseline (Tab. 3). We extend this comparison to HunyuanVideo at equal density in Appendix J. Figure 6 shows what a fixed rule misses: the first few layers and certain deeper layers stay dense longest, while density decreases over several intermediate steps rather than dropping at a single step. The best rule also changes with the budget, so we test the baselines at other budgets: ALLOT remains above the uniform baseline across budgets at 720p ×81 (1.3B and 14B, Appendix H). On 1.3B 480p ×33, it also outperforms the strengthened baseline with its warm-up re-searched at each of three budgets (Appendix J). 

5.5. Ablation study 

Each ablation replaces one component in the XAttention setup of Tab. 1 at ALLOT’s cost. Trajectory calibration is dominant: setting w(s) = 1 for every step lowers PSNR by 7.05 dB, and DiTFastAttn’s greedy rule on the same one-step errors reaches a similar 19.38 dB (Tabs. 1 and 4). A shared density across layers and a parametric shape rule also underperform, whereas density-proportional cost makes no significant difference after wall-clock alignment. Mean-pooled top-k ablations and an LPIPS-calibrated variant are reported in Appendix I.

Verifying the sensitivity estimate. The solver ranks den-sity tables by their estimated total sensitivity, the sum of ˆS

Number of calibration inputs. Cell probes use four prob-ing videos and trajectory calibration uses eight prompts. With a single probing video or calibration prompt, most cells keep the same dense/sparse status. ## A. Statistical Conventions and Timing 

A.1. Metrics and verdicts 

Ratios of final-video error are ratios of pixel MSEs. 

Verdicts. A win requires the lower bound of the paired PSNR interval to be > 0 and the mean LPIPS to be lower; a 

loss requires the upper bound of the PSNR interval to be < 0

and the mean LPIPS to be higher; everything else is a tie . The appendix tables additionally list DISTS [4] as a supplement only. Paired differences are ALLOT − baseline, followed by the 95% t-interval over prompts and the numbers of prompts on which ALLOT is better/worse. 

Trajectory gain. The trajectory gain is calibrated once per model family (Wan2.1 on 1.3B 480p ×33 with mean-pooled top-k, Wan2.2-A14B, and HunyuanVideo 480p ×33), and its window experiments are shared by all tables of that family across selection strategies, resolutions and model sizes. SVG2’s official pipeline uses a different flow shift, so its window experiments are rerun on that schedule. 

A.2. Timing and equal wall-clock time 

Wall-clock time is the per-video median in seconds, includ-ing text encoding, denoising and VAE decoding. Speedups are relative to each pipeline’s own dense time (the dense times are in the Dense row of Table 1 and in Appendix H). ALLOT’s wall-clock time is considered matched when it is no more than 1.01 × the baseline’s.

F.3. Latency tables of each path

F.5. Integration 

ALLOT keeps the selection strategy’s scoring and ranking, and the kernel is the same as the baseline’s (XAttention’s official kernel is replaced by FlashInfer on both sides, see §G.2; for Radial’s kernel paths see §H.3); only each cell’s cutoff is set to the density in the table. • XAttention: keeps blocks by block score up to the ta-ble’s density, replacing its per-head cumulative threshold. • SVOO: keeps key clusters in order of centroid score until the fraction of key tokens reaches the table value, bypassing its top-p threshold, its per-(layer, head) density clamping and its dense warm-up steps and first layer. • SVG2: likewise keeps key clusters in its ranking order until the fraction of key tokens reaches the table value, bypassing its top-p threshold, its minimum key-cluster ratio and its first-steps dense rule. • SpargeAttn: uses its built-in top-k entry. • Radial: takes the nearest reachable decay factor

Effect of density truncation. The effect of replacing threshold truncation with density truncation depends on the selection strategy. On the SVOO 480p ×33 pipeline, we preserve its default per-cell densities and dense-cell rules (§B.2) and change only the truncation rule. This lowers PSNR by 3.00 dB on 1.3B ( −3.00 [−3.53 , −2.46 ] 0/32) and by 1.96 dB on HunyuanVideo ( −1.96 [−2.69 , −1.24 ] 4/28). For XAttention, density truncation has the opposite ef-fect. At equal wall-clock time, ALLOT with density trun-cation exceeds its threshold-based counterpart by 4.50 dB (+4 .50 [+4 .00 , +5 .01 ] 32/0). The threshold-based variant assigns each cell a cumulative-mass threshold with the same cost as truncating that cell at its mean density. For a head with concentrated attention, a low threshold may retain only one or two blocks. Replacing each threshold in XAtten-tion’s default allocation with the density it induces raises PSNR by 2.27 dB ( +2 .27 [+1 .69 , +2 .85 ] 30/2) at 1.9% more wall-clock time. Even against this strengthened de-fault, ALLOT remains 5.98 dB higher at equal wall-clock time ( +5 .98 [+5 .46 , +6 .50 ] 32/0; §G.2).

G.2. XAttention and HEART 

Setting. Following the setting of HEART’s ablation [35] (Wan2.1-1.3B, 832 ×480 ×81, 50 steps, classifier-free guid-ance (CFG [8]) scale 5, UniPC, n=32 ), all settings keep the first 5 steps dense. Selection uses the official block esti-mation [24]. Attention uses the FlashInfer fixed 128-block block-sparse kernel [26]. On real inputs the two produce identical block masks. The budget is the cost of the default setting under the same latency table, 0.734 of dense. 

HEART’s EBC. We re-implement EBC from HEART’s description, without TMR (cross-step mask reuse): only one head at a time is sparsified to one of the thresholds 

{0.85 , 0.90 , 0.95 }, one step is sampled from each of four step segments to measure the frequency-weighted error of the CFG-combined velocity, and an integer program then picks one threshold per head such that the mean actual sparsity is no lower than that of a shared threshold of 0.90. Calibration uses 10 prompts that do not overlap with the evaluation set. EBC gives +1 .29 [+1 .00 , +1 .57 ] 32/0 over the default. 

HEART (EBC + TMR). Following HEART’s §4.2 and Algorithm 7.3, we implement TMR, which reuses masks across steps: for each layer, CFG branch and head, the token-wise means of query and key at the last re-scoring are stored together with that step’s block scores and mask; if the sum of the L1 drifts of the two at the current step does not exceed 

δTMR =30 (HEART’s default), the previous result is reused, otherwise the head is re-scored. When fewer than 40% of a layer’s heads need re-scoring, the whole layer reuses its masks; when more than 80% need it, the whole layer is re-scored; otherwise, heads are handled individually (HEART’s values). 

ScalingAttention (FAST). On EBC’s 10 calibration tra-jectories, FAST measures, for each layer, head and candidate density, the attention mass within the selected blocks and converts it to a Hellinger distance. A three-parameter fidelity surface A = β[1 − (1 − x)γ ] then sets a fidelity requirement for each (step, layer), where x = ( ℓ′ + ωt ′)/(1 + ω) and 

ℓ′, t ′ ∈ [0 , 1] are the normalized layer depth and noise level (1 for shallow layers and early steps), so the requirement is higher for shallow layers and early steps, following the de-scription in its main text; each head takes the sparsest density meeting it. γ and ω are grid-searched, β is set by bisection to meet the budget, and the combination maximizing the minimum fidelity is selected ( γ=4 , ω=1 ). γ=4 lies on the edge of the grid. ## I. Ablations 

I.1. Ablation on XAttention 

On XAttention, the parametric shape rule of Table 4 takes its shape from the table solved on XAttention: that table’s first 10 steps are fully dense, layer 0 is dense in 74% of the steps, and every other layer in less than 50%. The rule therefore keeps the first 10 steps and layer 0 dense over all steps, and lowers every other layer linearly with the step from 0.59 at step 10 to 0.05 at step 49, with the coefficient set by bisection on the latency table to match ALLOT’s cost. 

I.2. Density-proportional cost 

On the setting of Table 4 (XAttention, 1.3B 480p ×81, 

n=32 ), we keep everything else in ALLOT fixed, only change the cost in the solver to r(b) = b, and then align the budget to ALLOT’s cost using the latency table. The resulting table has mean density 0.48, yet its latency-table cost is 0.73 of dense. It has 446 dense cells (ALLOT 587), and its PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS do not differ significantly from ALLOT’s (Table I.1). 

I.3. Calibration metric of the trajectory gain 

Setting. 1.3B 720p ×81, SVOO pipeline, n=32 . The pixel-MSE calibration uses the table at a budget of 0.62 of dense: probes under SVOO’s selection strategy and the latency table of the SVOO path (§F.3). The LPIPS and DISTS calibrations differ only in the metric used to aggregate Mk.

Results. The pixel-MSE table gives 31.12 dB. Calibrating with LPIPS instead gives 30.28 dB ( −0.84 [−1.20 , −0.49 ]4/28); calibrating with DISTS gives 29.06 dB (−2.06 [−2.71 , −1.41 ] 1/31). Both alternatives also worsen LPIPS itself. Perceptual metrics saturate at large errors (LPIPS already reaches 0.52 in the first window, while MSE differs by about 460 ×), so they underweight early steps: the ratio of w over the first and last 10 steps is only 1/9 (LPIPS) and 1/29 (DISTS) of that under MSE calibration, and the densities of the early steps drop accordingly (dense cells 617 → 590 / 532). 

I.4. Ablation on mean-pooled top-k

## K. Robustness of the Measurement Setup 

K.1. Number of calibration inputs 

Each variant re-solves the table and compares it with the 4-video, 8-prompt baseline (Table K.1). With 2 probing videos, 96–99% of cells keep the same dense/sparse status across models. On XAttention (1.3B), 1 or 2 videos lose less than 0.6 dB, about 8% of ALLOT’s gain; 8 videos bring no significant improvement. Step-wise rankings are more sensitive to calibration inputs than layer-wise rankings: the single-prompt ratio of w over the first and last 10 steps ranges from 162 to 1507 on 1.3B (mean of 8: 333). 

K.2. Robustness of the trajectory gain 

All selection strategies reuse the trajectory gain measured with mean-pooled top-k (Table K.1). Window density changes the magnitude of w but barely its shape; calibrating separately at three densities changes 0.6% of the cells and PSNR by −0.02 [−0.10 , +0 .06 ] 15/17. Re-calibrating w

on 8 held-out prompts gives a log correlation of 0.999 with the original. When the 12 tables are re-solved with this w,98.4–99.3% of the cells retain their dense/sparse status. 

K.3. Probe resolution 

All 81-frame tables (720p and 480p ×81) are solved from 480p ×33 probes, except Radial 1.3B which uses 720p latent probes (§H.3). SVOO’s co-clustering changes with resolu-tion, so on the SVOO pipeline (1.3B 720p ×81) the probes were re-measured from the dense 720p generations of 4 eval-uation prompts and the table re-solved with the recipe of that row of Table 2. The mean final-video PSNR ( n=16 ) in-creases from 30.43 to 30.48 dB, +0 .05 [−0.14 , +0 .25 ] 9/7, and the LPIPS and DISTS intervals both contain 0.

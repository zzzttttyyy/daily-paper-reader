## Abstract 

Diffusion models, particularly Diffusion Transformers, achieve strong image and video generation quality but remain expensive at inference time due to repeated denoiser evaluations. Feature caching offers a deployment-friendly acceleration strategy by reusing or predicting intermediate representations without retraining the generator or modifying the sampler. However, existing cache-then-forecast methods often rely on Taylor-style finite-difference extrapolation, which can be-come unstable over longer cache intervals, and typically predict local module outputs whose errors may accumulate through subsequent denoiser blocks. We propose BeziCast , a training-free diffusion acceleration framework that forecasts output-proximal denoising representations using low-order Bézier trajectories. Bez-iCast estimates Bézier control points via Tikhonov-stabilized fitting, providing a smooth, capacity-controlled temporal parameterization that avoids explicit high-order derivative estimation and decouples trajectory capacity from cache-query density. To moderate aggressive extrapolation, we further derive equivalent fore-casting weights and introduce a convex-hull-inspired guardrail that detects high-risk predictions and softly pulls them toward a conservative simplex-projected estimate. Extensive evaluations across advanced image and video diffusion models demon-strate the effectiveness of BeziCast. In particular, BeziCast accelerates FLUX.1 by up to 4.79 × and HunyuanVideo by up to 4.11 ×, while preserving substantially better generation quality than competing acceleration baselines. 

## 1 Introduction 

Diffusion models[11], especially large generative models based on Diffusion Transformer[31, 4, 52, 14], have become a standard approach for high-fidelity image and video generation. Their sampling process, however, still requires repeated denoiser evaluations across many timesteps, and this inference cost remains a major obstacle to large-scale deployment. A central question in diffusion acceleration is how to approximate the original multi-step sampling process under a limited NFE budget[24, 46]. Existing approaches can be broadly grouped into three directions. Solver-based methods improve the sampler through higher-order updates[24, 25, 46], timestep schedules[13], and trajectory-aware error correction or time allocation[50, 39, 3, 51, 54, 38, 49, 29]. Distillation-based methods compress multi-step sampling into few-step or one-step generation through progressive distillation[33], consistency modeling[26], or distribution matching[45]. Feature caching methods instead keep the pretrained generator largely unchanged and reduce the cost of each denoiser evaluation by reusing or predicting internal representations[27, 20]. Compared with the former two directions, feature caching is particularly deployment-friendly because it avoids retraining the full model and does not require redesigning the sampling dynamics.

SpeCa[21] adds a lightweight verifier to accept or reject predicted features. Spectrum[9] takes a global function-approximation view by fitting Cheby-shev polynomial coefficients for final-layer denoiser features and deriving a horizon-independent error bound un-der its approximation setting. These methods show that denoiser represen-tations have exploitable temporal reg-ularity, but they also leave two design choices: which representation should be predicted, and which temporal ba-sis should be used. The first design choice is the temporal basis . Taylor-style predictors are simple, but finite-difference derivative estimates and local power-basis extrapolation can amplify small cache perturbations at longer offsets or higher orders[20, 16, 7], as evidenced by the instability of the blue curve and its large deviation from the ground truth on the left of Fig. 1. This sensitivity is consistent with the numerical behavior of monomial and Vandermonde-type systems[35], the non-monotone feature trajectories discussed by Feng et al.[7], and the large-horizon error growth observed by Han et al.[9]. At the same time, diffusion sampling trajectories often exhibit structured low-dimensional geometry[2]. We therefore seek a compact temporal parameterization that avoids explicit high-order finite differences while remaining analytically evaluable at cache steps. The second design choice is the forecasting target . Existing caching methods often predict attention outputs, MLP outputs inside transformer blocks[34, 28, 53, 21]. These local targets give fine-grained control, but their errors still pass through downstream denoiser blocks before reaching the final noise estimate and may accumulate across predicted modules. We instead predict an output-proximal denoising representation, which makes the path from representation error to denoising error shorter and more direct without assuming that this representation is intrinsically easier to forecast. Based on these observations, we propose BeziCast , a training-free Tikhonov-stabilized Bézier repre-sentation forecasting framework, achieving faster, more stable, and more accurate acceleration than competing methods , as shown on the right of Fig. 1. BeziCast predicts an output-proximal de-noising representation with a low-order Bernstein/Bézier trajectory whose control points are estimated by Tikhonov-stabilized fitting. The Bézier form provides analytic evaluation and a compact capacity-controlled function family[6, 40, 30], while the Bernstein basis has classical stability properties on a fixed interval[5], as shown by the orange curve on the left of Fig. 1, which is closer to the ground truth than Taylor-based extrapolation. Because cache queries are often short-range extrapolations, BeziCast does not use the convex-hull property as a guarantee; instead, it uses equivalent weights to detect signed or non-affine extrapolation and pulls high-risk predictions toward a simplex-projected conservative estimate. The pretrained denoiser and sampler remain unchanged. Our contributions are as follows: (1) We formulate training-free cache-then-forecast acceleration as output-proximal representation forecasting, avoiding multiple local module targets. (2) We use a low-order Bernstein/Bézier trajectory with Tikhonov-stabilized control-point fitting, replacing finite-difference Taylor extrapolation, decoupling trajectory capacity from cache-query density, and expressing each prediction as a regularized linear combination of cached representations. (3) We introduce an equivalent-weight convex-hull guardrail that detects aggressive extrapolation through negative weight mass, deviation from unit sum, and the ℓ1 norm, then pulls high-risk predictions toward a simplex-projected conservative estimate.

Sampler-based Acceleration. Sampler-based methods reduce the number of function evaluations by improving the reverse diffusion discretization. DDIM [36] enables deterministic non-Markovian sampling from DDPM [11] models with fewer steps. DPM-Solver [24] and DPM-Solver++ [25] derive high-order ODE solvers for probability-flow sampling, UniPC [46] unifies predictor-corrector updates, and EDM [13] improves noise parameterization, preconditioning, and sampling schedules. Recent trajectory-aware methods further exploit path regularity, error correction, or time allocation, including AMED-Solver [50], Trajectory Regularity [3], Simple and Fast Distillation [51], PAS [39], PFDiff [38], fast-sampling analysis [49], and BézierFlow [29]. These methods mainly accelerate sampling by changing the solver, schedule, or trajectory correction strategy. 

Training-based Acceleration. Training-based methods compress a multi-step teacher into a faster generator through extra optimization. Progressive Distillation [33] halves sampling steps iteratively; Consistency Models [37] and Latent Consistency Models [26] enforce trajectory-consistent pre-dictions for few-step generation; DMD [45] and DMD2 [44] match the teacher distribution rather than the full teacher trajectory; and InstaFlow [22] uses rectified-flow-style training to straighten text-to-image generation paths. These approaches can be effective, but they usually require additional training, teacher models, or specialized objectives. 

Feature-caching-based Acceleration. Feature-caching methods[20, 42, 34, 43] reduce inference cost by exploiting temporal redundancy in denoiser activations while keeping the pretrained denoiser largely unchanged. Early cache-then-reuse methods directly reuse intermediate features or residuals: DeepCache [27] reuses high-level U-Net features, FORA [34] extends fast-forward caching to dif-fusion transformers, ToCa [55] introduces token-wise feature caching, and Reuse and Diffuse [8] plus MagCache [28] study feature or residual reuse for video diffusion. Recent cache-then-forecast methods model feature evolution instead of copying old features: TaylorSeer [20] uses finite differ-ences and Taylor expansion, FreqCa [19] adds frequency-aware caching, Forecast-then-Calibrate [47] introduces feature calibration, HiCache [7] replaces Taylor-style monomial forecasting with Hermite-polynomial-based prediction, and Spectrum [9] fits global spectral approximations. Adaptive variants such as TeaCache [18], DiCache [1], SpeCa [21], and token-level cache updates [48] further show that diffusion features exhibit strong temporal and structural redundancy. BeziCast follows the training-free cache-then-forecast line, but focuses on output-proximal representation forecasting with a Tikhonov-stabilized Bézier trajectory and an equivalent-weight guardrail for extrapolation risk.

xs+1 = Ψ s(xs, ϵ s), (1) where Ψs denotes one solver step. We seek to reduce expensive denoiser computation while keeping both the pretrained denoiser ϵθ and the sampler update Ψs unchanged. A key design choice in cache-then-forecast acceleration is the representation to cache and predict. Prior works typically forecast local features such as attention, MLP, or residual outputs within transformer blocks [34, 28, 41, 21], but errors in these intermediate targets are propagated through subsequent nonlinear layers and can accumulate if multiple predictions occur per denoising step. To ensure errors are directly reflected in the final noise estimate and to simplify control, we forecast a single output-proximal representation immediately before the final output map, aligning with the final-block forecasting setting of Spectrum [9]. 

Definition 2 (output-proximal representation). We decompose the pretrained denoiser into a backbone and a final output map: 

zs = Fθ (xs, t s, c ), ϵθ (xs, t s, c ) = Oθ (zs, t s, c ). (2) Here zs ∈ RD is the flattened output-proximal denoising representation, and D absorbs token and channel dimensions. The map Fθ denotes the expensive backbone computation up to this representation, while Oθ denotes the lightweight output map that converts zs into the denoising prediction. The output map is always executed, both at full steps and cache steps.

3.2 Tikhonov-Stabilized Bézier Forecaster 

The temporal basis determines how cached denoising representations are predicted at skipped timesteps. Taylor-style predictors estimate finite differences and extrapolate them in a Newton-form power basis. BeziCast uses a low-order Bernstein/Bézier trajectory with Tikhonov-stabilized fitting. The forecaster is specified below by local time normalization, a Bernstein basis, and a Tikhonov control-point estimate. Appendix A.1.1 gives the design motivation and perturbation discussion. Since future full-step representations are unavailable during online inference, cache-step prediction is typically performed by extrapolating beyond the recent history window. The standard Bézier convex-hull property only holds inside the interpolation interval, so we control extrapolation by clipping the query coordinate with umax and by applying the equivalent-weight guardrail in Section 3.3.

## 4 Experiments 

We conduct comprehensive experiments on two representative generative tasks using SOTA mod-els, covering text-to-image generation with FLUX.1-dev[4] and text-to-video generation with HunyuanVideo[14]. Each task follows standard evaluation protocols and widely adopted benchmarks, enabling fair and reproducible comparisons with existing methods. We present additional BeziCast configurations and component-level ablations in separate analysis subsections. 

4.1 Experimental Setup Models and Tasks. For text-to-image generation, we evaluate BeziCast on FLUX.1-dev [4] using a 50-step reference schedule and the 200-prompt DrawBench suite [32], generating images at 

1024 × 1024 . For text-to-video generation, we use HunyuanVideo [14] and follow the official VBench [12] prompt set and evaluation protocol. 

Baselines and Metrics. We compare BeziCast with representative training-free acceleration meth-ods, including κ-step sampling, cache-reuse methods FORA [34], ToCa [55], and TeaCache [18], as well as forecasting-based methods TaylorSeer [20] and HiCache [7]. We use N for the cache interval, n for the number of Bézier control representations, and λ for Tikhonov regularization. We report sampling latency (per-image/video) and speedup relative to the original 50-step sampler, together with task-specific quality metrics: PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, ImageReward, and CLIP Score [10] for text-to-image generation; PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, and VBench Quality Score for text-to-video generation. 

4.2 Main Comparisons. Text-to-Image Generation. The left part of Table 1 reports the quantitative results on FLUX.1-dev. A.1 Why Use a Tikhonov-Stabilized Bernstein Basis? A.1.1 Design Motivation of Tikhonov-Stabilized Bernstein Basis 

The temporal basis determines how a cache-then-forecast method turns a short history of computed denoising representations into predictions at skipped timesteps [20, 7, 9]. Taylor-style predictors estimate local finite differences and extrapolate them in a Newton-form power basis [20, 7]. This design is effective for short horizons, but it couples two sources of sensitivity: finite differences first form alternating high-order derivative estimates from nearby cached features, and extrapolation then multiplies these estimates by coefficients that grow with the forecast offset and expansion order. Proposition A.1 in Appendix A.1 makes this mechanism explicit: for an order-p backward finite-difference predictor at future offset q, bounded cache perturbations are amplified by a factor upper bounded by Ppj=0 | qj

|2j in Eq. (24), whose leading term grows as a degree-p polynomial in 

q.BeziCast replaces direct reuse and high-order finite-difference extrapolation with a regularized local trajectory fit. It estimates a low-order Bernstein/Bézier curve from recent output-proximal representations and evaluates the fitted curve at cache-step coordinates. A Bézier curve is defined by a small set of control points and Bernstein basis functions [6]; once the control points are fixed, the curve can be evaluated analytically at any normalized time coordinate [30]. Thus, the number of control points sets trajectory capacity, while the cache schedule only determines how often the trajectory is queried. 14 Cache forecasting differs from standard Bézier interpolation because future full-step representations are unavailable during online inference. At a cache step, the method observes only previously computed full steps, so the query is often a short-range extrapolation beyond the most recent full step, i.e., u > 1. We therefore do not use the in-interval convex-hull property of Bézier curves as a direct correctness guarantee. Instead, we use the Bernstein/Bézier form as a smooth low-order trajectory family and explicitly control extrapolation through Tikhonov regularization, query-range clipping, and the equivalent-weight guardrail introduced in Section 3.3. The Bernstein basis provides the geometric structure behind this design. On the standard interval 

u ∈ [0 , 1] , Bernstein basis functions are nonnegative and form a partition of unity, so the correspond-ing Bézier curve lies in the convex hull of its control points [30]. Farouki and Goodman further show that the Bernstein basis has optimal stability among nonnegative polynomial bases on a fixed interval [5]. Although cache prediction may query u > 1, these properties remain useful as a diag-nostic lens: after Tikhonov fitting, each prediction can be written as an equivalent linear combination of historical representations. If the equivalent weights become heavily signed or deviate from an affine combination, the forecast is relying on aggressive extrapolation rather than a conservative combination of observed features. This observation motivates both the regularized fitting below and the coefficient-space guardrail that follows [15, 17].

We first ablate the representation used as the forecasting target. To isolate the target choice, we keep the pretrained FLUX denoiser, sampler, cache schedule, history length, Bernstein order, Tikhonov coefficient, and guardrail configuration fixed, and vary only the predicted representation. We compare three target types. Block-wise forecasting applies the same Bézier forecaster to local transformer-block outputs, resembling local-module feature caching where prediction errors are injected before subsequent blocks. Residual forecasting predicts the block residual/update represen-tation, following the residual-style target used in prior caching methods. In our implementation, the residual is defined as the difference between the block output and the block input. A.3 Warm-up and Fixed-period Full-step Schedule 

This subsection first defines the deterministic full-step schedule used by BeziCast, and then reports an ablation on the warm-up length. The schedule has two roles: the initial warm-up provides real output-proximal representations for the first local fit, and the later fixed-period full steps refresh the history used by cache-step forecasting. 

A.3.1 Method Details 

BeziCast uses a warm-up plus fixed-period full-step schedule. The warm-up length W is the number of initial solver steps evaluated by the full denoiser, and the fixed period R specifies how often a full denoiser evaluation is inserted after warm-up. At full steps, BeziCast computes and caches the true output-proximal representation; at cache steps, it predicts the current representation from the most recent cached full-step representations. For a determined or over-determined control-point fit, the warm-up length should provide at least as many historical full-step representations as the number of Bernstein basis functions. Let n denote the number of Bézier control representations. The default well-posed setting therefore uses 

W ≥ n. (70) In the main configuration, BeziCast uses a cubic Bernstein/Bézier forecaster with n = 4 , matching the control-representation notation used in the experimental tables: 

b0,3(u) = (1 − u)3, b1,3(u) = 3 u(1 − u)2,b2,3(u) = 3 u2(1 − u), b3,3(u) = u3. (71) Thus the default well-posed cubic setting uses W ≥ 4. The ridge system is still algebraically defined when fewer than n full-step representations are available, because the Tikhonov term makes the normal matrix invertible. We therefore also evaluate the aggressive W = 3 setting in the ablation below, where the goal is to measure the speed–fidelity trade-off near the well-posedness boundary. After warm-up, full denoiser evaluations follow a fixed period. We use the same zero-indexed solver convention as the main method, s ∈ { 0, . . . , T − 1}. Let R be the full-step period after warm-up, meaning that one full denoiser evaluation is performed every R solver steps. The full-step set is 

Sfull = {0, . . . , W − 1} ∪ { s ∈ { W, . . . , T − 1} : ( s − W + 1) ≡ 0 (mod R)} , (72) and the cache-step set is 

Scache = {0, . . . , T − 1} \ S full . (73) At a cache step s, the predictor uses only the most recent full-step representations: 

Is = Last Kmax ({i ∈ S full : i < s }) , (74) where Kmax is the maximum history length. Unless otherwise stated, we set Kmax = 6 . A.4 Online Inference Algorithm 

Algorithm 1 summarizes inference. We first construct the warm-up plus fixed-period full-step set. Full steps run the original denoiser and update the cache. Cache steps compute the equivalent ridge-Bézier weights, optionally blend with the convex-hull fallback, and pass the predicted output-proximal representation through the unchanged output map. The Ks < n branch is a fallback branch; under the default requirement W ≥ n, cache prediction starts after enough full-step representations are available.

Let D be the flattened representation dimension, Ks ≤ Kmax the available history length at cache step s, and n the number of Bézier control points. At one cache step, building the design matrix costs O(Ksn). Solving the n × n ridge system costs O(n3), and this part can be precomputed when the cache schedule and hyperparameters are fixed. The main high-dimensional operation is the weighted sum ωλ(us)⊤Z, which costs O(KsD). The guardrail adds a simplex projection in Ks dimensions, costing O(Ks log Ks) with a sorting-based algorithm, and one additional weighted sum. The memory overhead is O(Kmax D) for cached output-proximal representations. Algorithm 1 does not store the control representations persistently; prediction uses equivalent historical weights. Our experiments on both FLUX and HunyuanVideo were run on NVIDIA H20 GPU. 

Relation to existing predictors. Direct reuse is the zeroth-order case ˆzs = ziK . Taylor-style forecasting estimates local finite differences and extrapolates them in a power basis. Spectral predictors fit global polynomial bases over the denoising trajectory. BeziCast uses a local function-approximation form: it fits a short-history Bernstein/Bézier trajectory with Tikhonov regularization, evaluates it at cache steps, and writes the result as equivalent weights over historical output-proximal representations. The control-point number n is chosen independently of the cache period. 

Limitation. BeziCast is a training-free acceleration method and therefore preserves the pretrained denoiser and sampler, but its effectiveness depends on the temporal regularity of output-proximal denoising representations; when the denoising trajectory changes abruptly, aggressive cache intervals may still introduce prediction errors despite Tikhonov stabilization and the convex-hull guardrail. Its speed–quality trade-off is also sensitive to schedule and hyperparameters such as the warm-up length, cache period, Bézier control-point number, and regularization strength, which may require task- or model-specific tuning. In addition, our experiments mainly cover FLUX.1 for text-to-image generation and HunyuanVideo for text-to-video generation, so further validation on more architectures, samplers, resolutions, longer videos, and stronger downstream metrics is needed to fully establish generality. Finally, BeziCast assumes access to a suitable output-proximal representation and still executes the output map at cache steps, which may limit its applicability or acceleration benefit for architectures where such representations are inaccessible or the output head is non-negligible in cost. 27

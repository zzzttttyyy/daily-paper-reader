Abstract 

Diffusion models rely on stochastic inputs, yet on finite-precision hardware, the "random-ness" they consume is realized as deterministic numerical orbits generated by pseudorandom rules. Accessible orbit structure can become a learnable input and affect both training and generation because the realized loss and its gradient depend on the concrete pseudorandom values consumed at each optimization step. A small multilayer perceptron predicts the next value of an orbit from its recent history, measuring general sequence predictability. A diffusion probe replaces real images with online random tensors while preserving the diffu-sion architecture and training objective, measuring whether the target system can exploit orbit structure. After controlling marginal statistics and screening out clear dynamical and finite-precision failures, the remaining orbits still produce markedly different diffusion losses and generation quality on MNIST and CIFAR-10. Both measures show strong rank correlations with macroscopic generation degradation, although their local rankings differ. After normalization by the IID baseline, the probe loss and the real-data diffusion loss approximately follow an empirical power law, with different exponents on the two datasets. These results suggest that a pseudorandom source is not only a distributional choice, but also a model-dependent structured input. 

# 1 Introduction 

A digital computer does not draw a new ideal random variable from an abstract distribution at every step. Instead, it produces a concrete numerical orbit from a deterministic rule, an internal state, and finite-precision arithmetic. This raises a model-dependent question: after basic marginal statistics have been controlled, can different pseudorandom orbits still contain structures that a neural network can learn or exploit? Our experiments show that changing the orbit used by a diffusion process can change the output from samples close to the IID baseline to blank images, regular textures, or unrecognizable patterns. In the same experiments, lower training loss often appears together with worse generation quality. The random source may therefore be more than an interchangeable implementation detail. Traditional studies evaluate pseudorandom number generators through period length, uni-formity, serial dependence, statistical unpredictability, and cryptographic security. Standard tools include NIST SP 800-22 and TestU01 [2, 3, 4]. Recent work has also examined how PRNG quality affects stochastic simulation and machine-learning tasks. Severe statistical defects can damage downstream results, while several high-quality generators perform similarly in some settings [25, 26]. These studies mainly connect generator-level statistical quality with task performance. Another line of work uses neural networks to predict nonlinear or pseudorandom sequences. Neural networks can recover hidden relations from some pseudorandom data, and recent studies 1arXiv:2608.02575v1 [cs.LG] 3 Aug 2026 show that Transformers can predict linear congruential and PCG-type sequences [5, 6, 7, 21]. These results show that a sequence that appears random under conventional tests is not necessarily unlearnable for a neural network. However, this literature usually treats the random sequence itself as the prediction target. It does not study what happens when the same type of orbit enters a normal learning system as an internal random input. Noise design is also known to affect diffusion models. Previous work has studied non-Gaussian noise, non-isotropic Gaussian noise, blue noise, and the effect of inference seeds [8, 9, 10, 11]. These studies mainly modify the noise distribution, covariance, spectrum, or initial condition. We focus instead on the concrete pseudorandom orbit consumed throughout training and sampling. This makes it possible to study both whether an orbit can be predicted in a separate sequence task and whether it can be exploited inside the target diffusion system. We study two forms of model-relative orbit learnability. The first is next-value predictability: whether a small multilayer perceptron can predict the next orbit value from a continuous history. The second is exploitability in the target diffusion system: whether the orbit can help a diffusion model reduce its training objective even when random-number prediction is not the task. We measure the second property with a diffusion probe that keeps the diffusion network, noising process, and training objective, but replaces real images with online random tensors that contain no stable semantic structure. The MLP therefore measures direct sequence predictability, while the probe measures the response of the target learning system. We evaluate deterministic Logistic, finite-memory Markov-type, Sine and ShiftedSine orbits, with an IID source as the reference. The deterministic orbits are processed by empirical-rank uniformization, Box–Muller Gaussianization, and mean–variance calibration, while orbits with clear periodic structure, band alternation, or finite-precision absorbing states are excluded from continuous comparison. On MNIST and CIFAR-10, the remaining orbits still produce clearly different diffusion losses and generation quality. Both the MLP loss and the probe loss show strong rank correlations with macroscopic generation degradation, although their local rankings are not identical. After normalization by the IID baseline, the probe loss and the real-data diffusion loss approximately follow an empirical power law, with different fitted exponents on the two datasets. Preliminary classification and variational-autoencoder experiments further suggest that sensitivity to the random source depends on the learning system. The main contributions of this work are as follows. 1. We formulate the realized training objective as jointly dependent on the model, data, and concrete pseudorandom stream, revealing how accessible orbit structure can become learnable. 2. We treat the PRNG output used during diffusion training and sampling as a concrete numerical orbit and study its model-relative learnability beyond conventional generator-level statistical quality. 3. We distinguish general next-value predictability from exploitability inside the target diffusion system, and measure them using direct MLP prediction and a diffusion probe. 4. We construct an experimental framework that includes empirical-rank uniformization, Gaussianization, mean–variance calibration, continuous-stream control, and screening for strong dynamical structures and finite-precision failures. 5. On MNIST and CIFAR-10, both neural measurements are strongly associated with genera-tion degradation. The IID-normalized probe loss and real-data diffusion loss also show a learning-system-dependent empirical power-law relation. 

# 2 Optimization View of Pseudorandom Inputs 

In conventional machine-learning formulations, the objective is often written as a function of the model parameters and the data, L(θ; D), where D denotes the training data and θ denotes the 2model parameters. Random variables used during training are usually represented through an expectation, so that the idealized objective may be written as 

L(θ; D) = ER

Here, R represents the random variables involved in data ordering, diffusion-timestep selection, training noise, and other stochastic operations. An actual training step, however, evaluates only one realized sample of this expectation. It consumes concrete values from a pseudorandom stream. If Rt denotes the part of the stream used at step t, the realized objective at that step is ̂

Lt(θt; D, R t).

Its gradient therefore also depends on the realized pseudorandom values, 

∇θ̂ Lt(θt; D, R t).

Schematically, a gradient-based parameter update can be written as 

θt+1 = θt − ηt∇θ̂ Lt(θt; D, R t).

Thus, the concrete pseudorandom stream enters every optimization step through the realized loss and its gradient. If the stream contains accessible structure that helps reduce the objective, the same loss-minimization process used to learn regularities in the data may also favor parameter updates that exploit regularities in the pseudorandom orbit. In this sense, pseudorandom streams can act as learnable inputs to the optimization process rather than merely as implementation-level sources of stochastic variation.

We therefore use a fixed reference orbit of length 100000 to apply an empirical-rank uniformization to each deterministic orbit. The resulting values are then transformed by the Box–Muller method, and their output mean and standard deviation are fixed using an independently generated calibration sample of size 200000 [20]. This procedure controls the one-dimensional uniform marginal of each deterministic orbit and makes the mean and variance of the Gaussianized outputs comparable. It reduces differences caused by the raw marginal distribution and scale. Because the Box–Muller transformation uses adjacent orbit values, different sources can still retain different higher-order marginal structure and joint temporal structure. The present experiments compare the complete structure induced by each concrete orbit after these low-order controls. The IID baseline uses NumPy’s default output directly and is not additionally discretized by empirical ranks. All random sources run as continuous streams. Caches are used only to improve generation speed. After a cache is exhausted, the recurrence continues from the current state; old values are not replayed. Data order, diffusion timesteps, training noise, initial sampling noise, and stepwise sampling noise use separate orbits. Within the same run, models for all tested sources start from identical parameters, so the tested source does not affect model initialization in the diffusion experiments. 

3.2 Diffusion models and training budgets 

The MNIST experiments use all 60000 training images without data augmentation. Pixel values are mapped to [ −1, 1]. The model is based on the unconditional training workflow in Hugging Face Diffusers and is adapted to 28 × 28 single-channel images. It uses a three-level U-Net with channel widths (64 , 128 , 128) . (6) The model is trained for 30 epochs with a batch size of 128. The CIFAR-10 experiments use all 50000 training images, also without data augmentation [17]. The U-Net follows the public configuration of google/ddpm-cifar10-32 , with channel widths (128 , 256 , 256 , 256) , (7) but no pretrained parameters are loaded [19]. The training budget is fixed at 30000 optimization steps with a batch size of 64, which is approximately 38.4 full passes through the dataset. The Hugging Face training code is presented as an example that may be modified for a specific task. We use its framework and the public network configuration, but do not reproduce the full training duration of the public model [18]. Both datasets use 1000 forward diffusion timesteps and a noise-prediction mean squared error objective [1]. After training, 1000 images are generated with 100-step stochastic DDIM sampling with η = 1 [24]. To keep the random-source definition consistent between training and generation, the initial sampling noise and stepwise sampling noise are produced by the same type of tested source as in training, but by continuous orbits whose states are not shared with the training streams. In a practical generation system, the source type is normally not changed between training and sampling only to isolate mechanisms. Such a change would define a different random-source configuration. The main experiments therefore measure the overall effect of one consistent source configuration on the complete training–generation pipeline. The separate contributions of different random roles are left for later ablation studies. 4The shortened training budgets are not intended to reproduce the best public generation quality. 3.3 Basic observations and generation grades 

We fixed the model architecture, initial parameters, dataset, and training budget. We also applied the same statistical normalization to all deterministic orbits. Even under these controls, MNIST and CIFAR-10 still showed clear differences in the generated samples. Some sources produced images close to the IID baseline. Others produced only textures, lines, blurred outlines, or complete collapse. Thus, controlling the one-dimensional uniform marginal and the mean and variance of the Gaussianized output is not sufficient to make deterministic random orbits equivalent in diffusion training. We do not use one automatic image metric as the main measure of macroscopic generation quality. The observed outputs span blank or solid-color images, regular textures, unstructured noise, partial contours, severe distortions, and clear class-consistent images. Metrics such as MMD, class entropy, classifier confidence, or image entropy each measure only part of distributional distance, class balance, recognizability, or diversity. For example, unstructured noise can have high pixel or class entropy without having high generation quality. A single automatic metric therefore does not describe the large and heterogeneous quality range in these experiments. # 4 Pre-screening of random orbits 

Not every dynamical orbit is suitable for a continuous comparison of learnability. We first remove two classes of orbits with clear discrete structure or numerical failure. This prevents a few extreme cases from dominating the correlations among the MLP, the diffusion probe, and the human grade, and from entering the later power-law fit. 

4.1 Strong structure intrinsic to the dynamical system 

The first class includes stable periodicity, split support, long-term inaccessible intervals, or deterministic alternation between bands. Logistic 3.6 is a typical example. Its long-term orbit lies mainly in two separated intervals and alternates between the two bands in an approximate period-two pattern [12]. An empirical-rank map changes numerical marginals but not temporal order. The original alternation between a high band and a low band can become an approximate alternation of positive and negative signs after Gaussianization. Thus, even when the histogram, mean, and variance look normal, L3.6 contains a strong discrete structure created directly by deterministic band alternation. It is not part of the continuous learnability differences that we intend to compare. We keep L3.6 as a separate failure case, but exclude it from the two neural characterizations and from the probe power-law fit. 

4.2 Finite-precision absorbing states

The remaining orbits are characterized continuously in the next section. 95 Neural characterization of orbit predictability and diffusion exploitability 

The preceding experiments show that deterministic orbits can produce different diffusion losses and generation quality even when model initialization, training budget, and random-source processing are fixed. This suggests that the concrete orbit contains deterministic structure that can be exploited by a learning system. Orbit “learnability” is not an absolute quantity that can be defined independently of a model. We therefore use two neural tasks as operational characterizations. The first uses an MLP to predict the next value from a continuous orbit history and measures general next-value predictability. The second preserves the basic diffusion training mechanism but replaces real data with random tensors that have no stable semantic structure. It measures practical exploitability inside the target diffusion training system. The two tasks characterize general sequence predictability and system-specific response, respectively. We give them equal status in the comparisons below. Conventional indicators such as autocorrelation and Lyapunov exponents describe statistical dependence or dynamical instability, but they do not directly measure the exploitability of a concrete orbit for a specific learning system. Moreover, after empirical-rank and Box–Muller transformations, there is no simple one-to-one relation between dynamical indicators of the raw system and the Gaussianized sequence consumed by the model. We therefore begin with operational neural characterizations. A systematic comparison with traditional statistical and dynamical indicators is left for future work.

The MLP directly predicts the random orbit. The diffusion probe does not directly predict random numbers; it measures how easily orbit structure can be exploited by diffusion training.

5.1.2 Diffusion probe

In both the MNIST and CIFAR-10 probes, new clean random tensors are generated online for every batch. The training process no longer cycles through a pre-generated finite tensor pool. Within the same run, all tested sources use a reference clean-data stream with the same initial state, so they see the same sequence of online clean tensors. Validation and test tensors are generated from independent fixed seeds so that evaluation inputs are identical across sources within a run. The tested source still drives diffusion timesteps and training noise. The model cannot rely on class labels, contours, or stable semantic structure in real data, but orbit structure still enters the high-dimensional learning system through the diffusion objective. 5.2 Relation to human generation grades 

To test whether the two indicators reflect final generation quality, we compute their correlations with the human generation grades. Because of the wide quality range and the limitations of a single automatic metric discussed in Section 3.3, the ordered human grade is used as the variable for macroscopic degradation. It is used only for clear quality differences and rank correlation, not for fine distinctions among sources in the same grade. The grade is discrete and ordered, and several sources share the same grade. We therefore use Spearman rank correlation as the main statistic and Pearson correlation as a supplement. Spearman significance is computed by an exact two-sided permutation test that preserves the tie structure of the human grades. The analysis includes the 12 sources that pass the pre-screening and have both MLP and probe results. On MNIST, the probe’s Spearman correlation is about 0.0035 higher than the MLP correlation. On CIFAR-10, the MLP correlation is about 0.0036 higher than the probe correlation. The direction of the difference is not consistent across datasets, and both differences are below 0.004. The current results therefore do not establish a stable advantage for either method in detecting macroscopic degradation. The results show a strong association between general next-value predictability and macro-scopic generation degradation. An orbit that is easier for the simple MLP to predict from its history also tends to produce a lower diffusion probe loss and more severe generation degradation. However, the MLP and the probe measure different properties: general next-value predictability and practical exploitability in the target diffusion system. Their relative performance may change with model capacity, target-system scale, and evaluation task. The human grade cannot resolve order within the same grade. For example, several Logistic and Markov orbits receive the same CIFAR-10 grade. Different internal rankings by the MLP and the probe do not strongly affect the grade correlation. 5.4 Relation, cost, and limitations of the two characterizations 

The MLP test loss and diffusion probe loss have strongly correlated rankings. Their Spearman correlation is 

ρs = 0 .9231 (40) on MNIST and 

ρs = 0 .9161 (41) on CIFAR-10 using the matched run-1 probe. The two indicators therefore capture a substantial shared component: an orbit that is easier for a simple network to predict from its history is usually also easier for the diffusion training system to exploit. The two coefficients are close but not identical, and local source rankings differ. The methods are not equivalent. The MLP observes only a one-dimensional uniformized orbit and predicts the next value from the previous 32 values. The probe lets the source enter a high-dimensional U-Net through diffusion timesteps, noise tensors, and optimization. The MLP is therefore closer to local direct predictability, while the probe is closer to practical exploitability in a specific diffusion system. The previous quantities are operational measurements. Cost cannot be judged only from the number of model parameters. The MLP used here has only 273 parameters, but each source still requires 350000 training, validation, and test windows and as many as 5000 optimization steps. More importantly, the MLP capacity must be chosen in advance. If it is too large, several deterministic orbits can all be predicted to nearly zero loss. If it is too small, several orbits remain near the IID error. Both cases compress the useful dynamic range of the indicator. One probe training run is more expensive, but when the target diffusion model is already specified, its topology and training objective can be inherited directly from the target system. Model selection is then less arbitrary. The probe also has an empirical quantitative relation with the real diffusion loss, which has not been found for the current MLP indicator. The two methods are useful in different settings, and the modular probe also supports role-specific ablation studies: 1. If the main question is whether an orbit contains structure that can be predicted directly from a short history, the MLP is more direct. 2. If no target diffusion system has been selected and the goal is to compare general sequence predictability, a common MLP can be used. 3. If a target diffusion system is fixed and the goal is to estimate the effect of a source on its training loss, the diffusion probe is more appropriate. 4. If all orbits are close to the IID baseline, or if differences between proxy indicators are small, complete real-data training is still needed for confirmation. 5. If the goal is to identify which random role causes the observed effect, the probe can test one role at a time while keeping all other random roles at the operational IID reference. The current data do not establish the universal range of either indicator. We study only a limited number of Logistic, Markov-type, and ShiftedSine orbits and use one history length and one final MLP capacity. MNIST has three complete runs. The main CIFAR-10 power-law analysis has one matched real-data experiment and one matched online-probe experiment. Another CIFAR-10 probe run is used only to examine cross-run ranking stability and is not included in the power-law fit. More source families, architectures, training budgets, and datasets are needed to test the stability of the rankings and the probe power law. For a fixed target system, we place more emphasis on the diffusion probe. The MLP directly measures local next-value predictability of a one-dimensional orbit. The probe lets the random orbit enter a high-dimensional system through diffusion timesteps, noise tensors, and optimization, closer to the downstream task that we want to predict. In the current experiments, only the probe loss shows a stable empirical power law with real-data diffusion loss. This 17 advantage is limited to the tested model sizes and source set and does not establish that the probe is universally better than the MLP. 

# 6 Preliminary experiments outside diffusion models 

To examine whether similar random-orbit effects appear outside diffusion models, we also test an MNIST classification MLP and a convolutional VAE [16]. The classification MLP is different from the orbit-prediction MLP in Section 5: it performs standard image classification, while the latter predicts the random sequence itself. The VAE uses reparameterized sampling and the standard variational objective [23]. 

6.1 Classification MLP 

The classification experiment compares IID, Logistic, Markov-type, and ShiftedSine sources, for 12 sources in total. The classifier has one hidden layer and is trained for 20 epochs in three independent runs. The tested source controls model initialization and the training-data order in each epoch. The architecture and training budget are otherwise fixed. All final test accuracies are high. The sources produce small numerical differences, but these differences do not form a stable ranking across the three runs, and their magnitude is similar to the variation across runs. Under this classifier and training configuration, we therefore do not observe a stable effect of source replacement on final classification accuracy. The VAE experiment compares the same 12 sources, but only one complete run has been per-formed. # 7 Conclusions and future directions 

7.1 Main conclusions 

We study the interaction between pseudorandom sources and neural training systems from the viewpoint of deterministic orbits. At each optimization step, the realized loss and its gradient depend on the concrete pseudorandom values consumed at that step, providing a direct route 19 through which accessible orbit structure can influence learning. Even after deterministic orbits receive the same empirical-rank uniformization, Gaussianization, and mean–variance calibration, their concrete numerical trajectories can still produce very different training losses and outputs in a complete diffusion training and sampling pipeline. In addition to probability-distribution descriptions, it is therefore useful to treat the actual pseudorandom orbit as the output of a deterministic dynamical system and to study its structure together with its coupling to the learning system. We first define a pre-screening procedure for strong intrinsic dynamical structure and finite-precision failure. It separates periodicity, band alternation, and absorbing states from continuous learnability differences. We then characterize the screened orbits using MLP next-value prediction and a diffusion probe. Both indicators are strongly correlated with the ordered generation grades. Their broad rankings are similar, but some local rankings differ. This suggests both a general predictable component shared across learning systems and a task-dependent response. After normalization by the IID baseline, diffusion probe loss and real-data diffusion loss approximately follow an empirical power law on both MNIST and CIFAR-10, with clearly different fitted exponents. The relation is therefore better viewed as a calibration that depends on the model, training configuration, and data distribution, not as a universal property of the random source. Preliminary experiments outside diffusion models also show that a downstream MLP, a VAE, and a diffusion model have different sensitivities to random-source changes. The current evidence is not sufficient to establish a general law across model classes. 

7.2 Research directions suggested by this study 

1. Broader validation. Repeat the experiments with more generator families, parameters, initial states, numerical precisions, datasets, and larger models. On larger models, compare whether the MLP and the diffusion probe still produce similar source rankings and similar correlations with generation quality. 2. Random-source requirements at larger model scales. Test whether larger models and increasing numbers of random calls allow learning systems to exploit weaker orbit structures, and whether larger systems therefore require random sources that are harder to learn and exploit. 3. Scale-dependent tests of industrial PRNGs. Test commonly used machine-learning and industrial PRNGs in current and larger diffusion systems. Determine whether their generated orbits can already be exploited by present models, and how this risk changes as model scale and random-call budgets increase. 4. Random-source design for learning systems. Develop source-evaluation and source-design principles for artificial-intelligence systems in addition to conventional statistical tests. Study whether sources with lower model-relative exploitability improve robustness, and whether controlled temporal structures can benefit particular models or tasks. 5. Attribution of random-source effects. Use the modular probe to test specific random roles while keeping other roles at the operational IID reference. This can reveal how data order, timestep selection, training noise, and sampling noise contribute to loss and generation quality. 6. Stability of the two characterizations. Vary the MLP history length, model capacity, and training budget to avoid regimes in which all sources are either predictable or unpre-dictable. Also determine how small a compressed diffusion probe can be while preserving source rankings and pairwise relative exploitability. 7. The empirical power law. Test the empirical relation on more models and datasets. Study how its exponent depends on architecture, training budget, data complexity, random-role configuration, and numerical implementation. 8. Other model classes. Extend the experiments to other VAEs, GANs, Transformers, 20 and larger diffusion models. Study how probabilistic and dynamical descriptions jointly characterize random-source effects. 9. Can controlled non-IID structure outperform an operational IID reference? 

Compare operational IID references with non-IID stochastic processes that have controlled temporal structure. Test whether some controlled structures improve the performance or robustness of a specific learning system.

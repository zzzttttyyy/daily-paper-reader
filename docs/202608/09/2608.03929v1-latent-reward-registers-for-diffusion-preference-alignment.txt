ABSTRACT
Aligning diffusion models with human preferences usually relies on a sparse ter-
minal reward evaluated on the final generated samples, presenting a severe tem-
poral credit-assignment challenge across the multi-step denoising process. We
propose Latent Reward Registers, a mechanism that estimates terminal preference
directly from intermediate noisy latents by prepending learnable, position-free
register tokens to the input sequence of a frozen Diffusion Transformer (DiT).
This independent readout mechanism extracts latent reward evidence without al-
tering the generator’s hidden states or velocity field. The resulting dense, dif-
ferentiable reward signal throughout the full denoising process facilitates two
alignment strategies. For training, Reward-Gradient On-Policy Distillation (RG-
OPD) distills reward-guided updates along on-policy trajectories, bypassing the
computationally expensive rollouts of standard policy gradients. For inference,
Reward-Guided Sampling (RGS) steers trajectories via magnitude-matched re-
ward gradients without parameter updates.
Empirically, at high noise levels
(u=0.8), the registers reach the highest pairwise accuracy among the evalu-
ated latent reward models. Furthermore, RG-OPD outperforms online reinforce-
ment learning baselines while reducing GPU hours by up to 33×, and RGS
establishes a new state-of-the-art among training-free methods, strictly enhanc-
ing both alignment and perceptual metrics. Code and weights are available at
https://github.com/Guanys-dar/latent-reward-register

INTRODUCTION
While diffusion and flow-matching models have achieved remarkable success in generating high-
fidelity visual content, aligning these models with human preferences remains a critical challenge.
Current methods generally rely on reward maximization (Lee et al., 2023; Clark et al., 2024; Liu
et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2026). An external reward model evaluates the final generated image, and
the generator is updated to maximize this scalar score, thereby improving perceptual quality and
prompt alignment.
Existing alignment methods typically fall into two paradigms. Reward backpropagation (Lee et al.,
2023; Wu et al., 2024; Clark et al., 2024; Prabhudesai et al., 2023) unrolls the sampling trajectory to
compute exact gradients from a differentiable reward model. While this enables fast optimization, it
often leads to mode collapse. Alternatively, online reinforcement learning (RL) (Black et al., 2023;
Liu et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2026) formulates generation as a sequential policy
optimized via policy gradients. This mitigates mode collapse but introduces high variance and low
sample efficiency.
Both paradigms face the same bottleneck: they rely on a single scalar reward evaluated only on
the fully generated image. Because this terminal score cannot be easily attributed to specific inter-
mediate steps, the supervision is severely delayed. To pass this delayed signal back, reward back-
propagation must unroll long generation chains in memory, making it prone to over-optimization.

Our central insight is that DiT internal representations already encode sufficient evidence about the
final output quality, well before the trajectory reaches a fully denoised state. Even under heavy noise,
these features capture emerging global structure, prompt alignment, and overall trajectory viability.
Rather than propagating a single endpoint score backward through a full rollout, we can therefore
estimate the expected terminal preference directly from each intermediate latent, turning sparse
terminal feedback into a dense, step-wise reward signal without requiring complex approximations.
Building on this insight, we introduce Latent Reward Registers, a non-intrusive mechanism that
turns a frozen DiT into an accurate, step-wise reward model. As illustrated in Fig. 1, a small set
of learnable, position-free register tokens is added as global readouts to aggregate reward-relevant
evidence from the intermediate representations of the frozen transformer. A lightweight readout
head then predicts the expected terminal preference directly from any intermediate latent. By dif-
ferentiating this prediction with respect to the latent, we obtain a local reward gradient at every
denoising step. Because the registers do not modify the generator’s hidden states or velocity predic-
tions, the pretrained generative dynamics remain fully intact. As a result, the framework provides
dense, differentiable reward supervision with minimal computation overhead.
This dense reward signal supports alignment during both training and inference.
For training,
Reward-Gradient On-Policy Distillation (RG-OPD) distills reward-guided updates along on-policy
trajectories, bypassing the computationally expensive rollouts of standard policy gradients. For
inference, Reward-Guided Sampling (RGS) steers trajectories via magnitude-matched reward gra-
dients without parameter updates, allowing multiple objectives to be composed at test time. Our
contributions are summarized below.
• Reliable latent reward signals. Latent Reward Registers estimate step-wise rewards directly
from intermediate noisy latents. Even at high noise levels, their pairwise ranking accuracy remains
competitive with external reward models that require fully denoised images.
• Efficient training-time alignment. By distilling register-guided on-policy trajectories, RG-OPD
surpasses online RL baselines in alignment quality while reducing GPU hours by up to 33×.
• Effective inference-time alignment. RGS steers trajectories using the derived latent reward gra-
dients without parameter updates, improving target rewards while maintaining a superior reward–
quality trade-off relative to existing training-free sampling methods.

RELATED WORK
Diffusion Preference Alignment.
Preference alignment for text-to-image generators is dominated
by training-time methods. Reward backpropagation (Lee et al., 2023; Clark et al., 2024; Prabhudesai
2


et al., 2023) differentiates through the denoising chain to optimize the generator directly, while on-
line reinforcement learning (Liu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2026) treats generation as a policy refined
with policy gradients. These training-time approaches inherently bind the model to a fixed objective
and incur heavy training costs. Furthermore, their reliance on sparse endpoint rewards complicates
credit assignment across denoising steps. Training-free alignment offers an inference-time alterna-
tive, but existing schemes incur substantial computational overhead, whether through iterative search
and optimization (Yeh et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025) or guidance gradients that become unstable at
high noise levels (Song et al., 2023). Our approach instead extracts a dense, differentiable step-wise
reward directly in the latent space, keeping alignment efficient at both training and inference time.
Latent Reward Models.
Most reward models operate on clean, decoded images (Xu et al., 2023;
Kirstain et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2025). Applying them to intermediate states would require forming a
clean estimate from each intermediate latent via expensive VAE decoding at every step. Moreover,
the resulting signals are noisy or unreliable on heavily corrupted inputs. Recent latent reward mod-
els instead evaluate noisy states directly, drawing evidence from selected internal features (Zhang
et al., 2025), early cross-attention maps (Cui et al., 2026), or separate process reward models (Mi
et al., 2025; Go et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2024). Existing methods typically rely on heuristic feature
engineering or computationally heavy auxiliary networks. Latent Reward Registers instead append
lightweight, position-free register tokens to a frozen DiT, establishing a differentiable reward mech-
anism that avoids manual feature selection and leaves the base generator unaltered. This design
directly supports both RG-OPD and RGS.
Test-time Guidance.
Guidance methods steer a pretrained generator without retraining it. Clas-
sifier guidance injects the gradient of an auxiliary classifier into the score estimate (Dhariwal &
Nichol, 2021), and classifier-free guidance (CFG) extrapolates between conditional and uncondi-
tional predictions to strengthen prompt adherence (Ho & Salimans, 2022); the latter is standard in
modern text-to-image diffusion. RGS continues this lineage: it leaves the generator and its native
text guidance untouched and appends a reward-gradient correction whose magnitude is matched to
the sampler’s own displacement. Because the correction is derived from latent registers at interme-
diate steps, sampling needs neither image decoding nor a separate reward-model backbone.
3
METHOD
To address the temporal credit-assignment problem in preference alignment, we construct a dense,
differentiable reward signal directly from intermediate latent states, providing a unified supervision
mechanism for both training and inference time alignment. The proposed framework consists of
three core components:
• Latent Reward Registers (Sec. 3.1): A lightweight mechanism that predicts the expected ter-
minal preference using learnable register tokens and frozen DiT features. This auxiliary stream
extracts step-wise reward signals from intermediate noisy latents without perturbing the original
velocity field (Fig. 2).
• Reward-Gradient On-Policy Distillation (Sec. 3.2): A training-time alignment strategy that
constructs detached one-step optimization targets along the student’s sampling trajectory. This
bypasses backpropagation through complete denoising chains and eliminates the high variance of
rollout-level policy gradients.
• Reward-Guided Sampling (Sec. 3.3): An inference-time alignment technique that steers latent
trajectories using magnitude-matched, step-wise reward gradients, requiring no additional param-
eter updates.

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate the framework along three axes: prediction accuracy of Latent Reward Registers on
noisy latents, training efficiency of RG-OPD against RL and reward backpropagation baselines,
and inference effectiveness of RGS relative to training-free sampling methods. Primary evaluations
use SD3-Medium, with FLUX.1-dev providing cross-architecture evidence; we then ablate register
designs and analyze noise robustness. Full protocols and extended analyses appear in the Appendix.
4.1
LATENT REWARD PREDICTION
Setup.
We append K=32 register tokens to a frozen SD3-Medium backbone, training only the
auxiliary branch. We measure pairwise preference accuracy across four benchmarks totaling 54,170
pairs: ImageReward (Xu et al., 2023), HPDv2 (Wu et al., 2023), HPDv3 (Ma et al., 2025), and
GenAI-Bench (Li et al., 2024). Accuracy is defined as the frequency with which a model assigns
a higher score to the human-preferred image within a held-out pair. Latent reward models, includ-
ing our method, evaluate paired latents forward-noised to the high-noise regime u=0.8. Standard
endpoint models (ImageReward, PickScore, HPSv2, HPSv3) score the clean decoded images, pro-
viding a cross-regime reference. We compare against five latent reward baselines: LRM (Zhang
et al., 2025) on SD1.5 and SDXL, PAVRM (Mi et al., 2025)(re-implemented on SD3-Medium),
Diffusion Probe (Cui et al., 2026), and DiNa-LRM (Liu et al., 2026). For fair comparison, Table 1
matches our backbone to the SD3-Medium baselines; Appendix C details FLUX.1-dev results.

ANALYSIS
Register design.
The persistent register state is the main driver of accuracy. Removing the register
causes the largest performance drop, while the skip-FFN design and the trainable query projection
each add complementary gains (Table 4). Reducing the number of readout layers, by contrast, has
little effect, indicating that persistent side-stream aggregation matters more than deep multi-scale
feature extraction.
Training efficiency.
Dense reward gradients provide continuous supervision throughout the de-
noising process, which accelerates convergence substantially relative to policy-gradient RL. On-
policy trajectories meanwhile track the evolving student distribution. As Figure 6 shows, RG-OPD
reaches Flow-GRPO-equivalent HPSv3 levels 14.0×–33.2× faster in GPU hours on both back-
bones. To enable high-frequency monitoring of training dynamics, the efficiency trajectories are
evaluated on a designated validation subset of 256 prompts. In contrast, the ultimate performance

E.2
INFERENCE COST OF REWARD-GUIDED SAMPLING
Reward Register has a memory footprint comparable to standard CFG and an inference la-
tency competitive with the most efficient guided baselines (Table 10). All reported numbers are
measured end-to-end on a single uncontended H800 GPU in bf16, generating 10242 images over 42
steps with CFG scale 4.5 and batch size 1. Each method runs in its own process and is averaged over
ten runs after ten warm-up runs. In the standard deployment the register shares the frozen denoiser
and text encoders of the sampler, so the memory overhead is limited to the 147.6 M trainable param-
eters of the read path and reward head: peak memory rises only from 16.9 GiB to 17.6 GiB versus
unguided CFG (a 1.04× factor). Baselines that require separate neural backbones instead consume
27–34 GiB. Computationally, each guided step adds roughly 50 ms and 5.9 TFLOPs on top of the
12.4 TFLOPs of a base generation step.
End-to-end latency is 1.80× that of standard CFG, slightly above PAVRM (1.67×) but far below
Diffusion Probe (2.63×) and the decode-then-ImageReward pipeline (4.12×). Restricting guidance
to the early phase (σi > 0.8, i.e., 18 of the 42 steps) lowers the overhead to 1.34× CFG while
keeping roughly 80% of the HPSv3 gains of the full scheduler.

E.5
ROBUSTNESS TO THE ODE SOLVER
The primary results in the main text are based on first-order Euler integration of the generative flow
ODE (Lipman et al., 2023). To verify whether RGS generalizes beyond this specific discretization,
we evaluate the RGS protocol detailed in Appendix B using four additional classical numerical
integrators (Hairer et al., 1993). These include the two-step Adams–Bashforth (AB2) method, which
is a multistep method requiring a single model evaluation per step analogous to Euler; the explicit
midpoint method; Heun’s method (a trapezoidal predictor–corrector); and the classical fourth-order
Runge–Kutta (RK4) method. RGS composes with higher-order solvers in the same way as with
Euler: the reward gradient is computed once at the beginning of each step, the solver’s internal
velocity evaluations use standard CFG forwards, and the reward-gradient correction from Sec. 3.3
is applied to the solver output afterwards. Its magnitude is scaled relative to the step displacement,
αi · RMS(∆z), with no hyperparameter re-tuning. For these experiments, we implement the three-
band RGS schedule from Sec. 3.3. The guidance strengths are set to αearly=0.30 for the high-noise
phase (σ>0.8) and αmid ∈{0.05, 0.10} for the mid-range phase (σ>0.2). The latter encompasses
both the standard deployed configuration and an augmented mid-band variant, while guidance is
omitted in the low-noise tail. The evaluation is conducted on a subset of 250 prompts from the
balanced benchmark, with two seeds per prompt. Generations are performed at 10242 resolution
over 42 steps with a CFG scale of 4.5 using single-head RGS. Each solver’s respective CFG baseline
and the two RGS configurations are evaluated on matched prompt–seed pairs. Because these runs
use a 250-prompt subset, the baseline CFG numbers deviate slightly from those in Table 3, which
uses the full 400-prompt set.

under every integrator: paired per-image HPSv3 rises by roughly two points over the corresponding
same-solver CFG baseline, with consistent improvements in the other optimized reward metrics and
in no-reference quality. The drift attributable to RGS stays controlled relative to each solver’s base-
